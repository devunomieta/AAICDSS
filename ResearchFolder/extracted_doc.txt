ABSTRACT

The increasing burden of tuberculosis (TB) and pneumonia, coupled with a shortage of radiologists and limited digital infrastructure, continues to challenge timely chest X-ray (CXR) interpretation in many low-resource healthcare settings, including Nigeria. Although artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated significant potential in medical imaging, many existing clinical decision support systems (CDSS) depend on cloud infrastructure, operate as opaque "black-box" models, and provide limited support for offline clinical workflows. This study presents AffiongAI, a microservice explainable system for chest X-Ray analysis, developed to support the classification and triage of adult chest X-ray images into three categories: Normal, pneumonia-suggestive, and TB-suggestive. The project focuses on the software engineering design, implementation, and evaluation of an integrated AI platform rather than the development of a new machine learning model. The system adopts an Agile development methodology and a microservices-based architecture to ensure modularity, maintainability, and scalability. MedRAX-2 serves as the orchestration framework for coordinating AI reasoning and tool execution, while a pre-trained TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 model performs chest X-ray classification. To improve transparency, Grad-CAM visualisations are generated to highlight image regions influencing model predictions, while Monte Carlo Dropout is employed to estimate predictive uncertainty and produce heuristic confidence bounds that support clinician interpretation. A locally hosted Llama 3 8B model, deployed through Ollama, generates constrained narrative reports from structured classification outputs, eliminating dependence on cloud-based large language models. The system further incorporates offline-first capabilities through a Progressive Web Application (PWA), local SQLite data persistence, audit logging, clinician override management, and governed synchronisation mechanisms to support deployment in environments with intermittent connectivity. The study concludes that uncertainty-aware, explainable AI systems can be effectively deployed on resource-constrained hardware without continuous internet connectivity through appropriate software engineering practices. AffiongAI contributes a reproducible framework for integrating local AI inference, explainability, governance, and offline resilience into clinical decision support systems suitable for low-resource healthcare environments. Future work should focus on large-scale clinical validation, prospective usability studies, optimisation for edge-computing hardware, and broader institutional deployment.

CHAPTER ONE

INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background to the Project

Chest diseases, particularly tuberculosis (TB) and pneumonia, remain major public-health concerns in Nigeria and across West Africa. Nigeria carries a substantial share of the global TB burden. According to the World Health Organization's (2024) report on TB, Nigeria had 4.6% of all new TB cases worldwide in 2023. That makes it one of the eight countries where the majority of global TB cases occur. At home, more than 360,000 TB cases were reported in 2023, which is up 26% from 2022. The 2025 WHO report indicates that approximately 510,000 new TB cases manifest annually, including 61,000 children. The problem is made worse by poverty, crowded homes, and high rates of HIV infection.

Pneumonia further strains the health system. Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) constitutes a significant cause of hospital admission and inpatient mortality in Nigeria, with intra-hospital mortality figures ranging from 7.4% to 26% across tertiary facilities (Adeoti et al., 2022). In a study of CAP in Ilorin, North-Central Nigeria, the condition accounted for 5.9% of all medical admissions, with Klebsiella pneumoniae as the predominant pathogen and an intra-hospital mortality rate of 17.6% (Otubu et al., 2023). However, despite these worrying statistics, many members of the public, especially those living in rural areas, lack timely access to diagnostic services needed to differentiate pneumonia, tuberculosis, and other pulmonary infections. The clinical overlaps, along with the lack of radiologists, result in direct preventable death due to delayed or erroneous diagnosis. Although a chest X-ray is not sufficient to determine the root causes of pneumonia or provide the full clinical picture, it still plays an important role in the diagnostic process, as it helps physicians identify radiological features that should be investigated, treated, tested, or reviewed by specialists.

There is a critical shortage of professionals working in radiology and diagnostic imaging in Nigeria. While the total doctor-to-population ratio in Nigeria is 2.9 per 10,000 people or 1 doctor per 3,474 patients, which is much lower than the recommended number of 17 per 10,000 people according to WHO (Nigeria Health Watch, 2025), the situation with radiologists is even worse. Large teaching hospitals in urban centers like Lagos, Ibadan, and Abuja see many people standing in line, and rural and community health facilities do not employ any radiologists. Instead, they rely on general practitioners to interpret results with little assistance (Idowu & Okedere, 2020). In such settings, general practitioners and radiographers may need to make early triage decisions before a radiologist is available.

The use of AI in medical imaging worldwide has generated considerable optimism about the potential to improve diagnostic capabilities and enhance clinical efficiency. Deep learning models have proven highly effective in detecting abnormalities in chest X-rays. In contrast, recent developments in large language models have created opportunities to build intelligent agents for medical reasoning that integrate multiple diagnostic tools (Elhaddad et al., 2024). Examples of such frameworks include MedRAX, which shows how image analysis tools and language interfaces can be combined. Yet one of the most problematic issues is that most medical-image AI applications are trained and validated on data from high-income settings. Variations in the patient population, disease prevalence, imaging devices, image acquisition methods, and labeling can adversely affect the model's performance when applied elsewhere. Convolutional neural networks, which were trained on large Western datasets of chest X-rays, turned out to underdetect disease in Black patients and people of low socioeconomic status (Seyyed-Kalantari et al., 2021), and tackling machine learning bias across high-income and LMIC settings requires more than just re-training the same model (Soltan et al., 2024). The differences between these geographic settings pose an extremely high risk of assuming that an application with high benchmark results would work properly in Nigeria as well. As these tools cannot be trusted to operate alone in resource-constrained environments, their use does not eliminate the need for clinical decision-making; rather, it suggests that the most appropriate use of this model is as an adaptive decision-support tool for classification and referral.

The problem of digital infrastructure represents another challenge. While some progress was made in increasing mobile broadband penetration, only approximately 23% of rural communities in Nigeria had internet access in 2024. Additionally, health facilities still face problems related to unreliable electricity supply, inadequate connectivity, and poor digital literacy (NCC, 2025). Thus, cloud-based medical AI systems might be hard to operate outside Nigeria's major cities due to limited infrastructure. Moreover, such systems may become expensive to obtain due to licensing costs and the requirement to use services provided by foreign companies priced in foreign currencies. This issue requires the development of software architectures that can continue to support clinical workflow regardless of connectivity availability and quality.

To address these challenges together, the AffiongAI project is introduced to address high disease burden, the lack of specialists to interpret chest X-ray images, uncertainty about the ability to adapt imported AI models to local conditions, and infrastructure limitations that make cloud-based solutions infeasible. The system works with adult chest X-rays and classifies them as either Normal, suggestive of pneumonia, or suggestive of tuberculosis. The project's role is to facilitate triage by helping users prioritize cases and communicate the uncertainty of predictions to direct them to a radiologist's examination.

The proposed system incorporates some elements of the MedRAX framework into a more limited software prototype. It combines the chest X-ray classifier pipeline with conformal prediction for uncertainty-aware results, Grad-CAM visualization for image-level explanations, and an offline-enabled user interface that maintains workflow when connectivity is lost. A locally trained language model could produce a short, structured story based on the classifier’s results. Still, it won’t be able to interpret the images or make any medical decisions on its own. Overrides by clinicians will be logged and stored for future governed reassessment and will not be automatically used for model retraining.

By performing experiments on publicly available chest X-ray datasets, including one from Nigeria with Normal, pneumonia, and TB classes, the present research aims to assess the feasibility of building a system that incorporates all the above-mentioned elements. The research neither claims prospective clinical validation nor the superiority over the radiologists, and does not promise to reduce morbidity or mortality in any way. However, it provides a software engineering artifact, along with the experimental framework, that could support further responsible development of locally relevant imaging systems.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

Chest x-ray interpretation is a critical aspect of evaluating respiratory diseases; however, timely interpretation is hampered by a shortage of specialized staff and limited access to diagnostic support tools. Radiologists, radiographers, and general practitioners are known to evaluate chest x-rays and triage during periods of heavy workload, reporting delays, and the absence of specialists. Despite the availability of several machine learning models in evaluating chest x-rays, many of these systems are neither designed nor assessed within the context of such conditions in resource-limited settings. This study will seek to address the issue of inadequate availability of cost-effective, locally applicable, interpretable, uncertainty-aware, and offline-capable chest x-ray classifier and triage system, evaluable using Nigerian-related open-source data. Four measurable gaps include:

Cloud-dependent and potentially costly CXR AI workflows: In commercial clinical AI solutions, reliance on remote servers, licence subscriptions, and constant connectivity is commonplace. All three factors impose restrictions on facilities operating with limited resources, unreliable connectivity and power supply. When a solution requires a reliable cloud-based service to function, connectivity issues will disrupt the process flow, thus requiring a specific design approach.

Limited transparency and poor communication of uncertainty: AI outputs often lack adequate transparency to ensure safe use in clinical settings. An AI output will predict but fail to show which areas of the image led to the prediction, explain the constraints on the prediction, or notify the user when the output should not be trusted until a review is conducted. Explainability can lead to automation bias or to clinicians rejecting the tool (Asan et al., 2020). Explainability is a vital issue in AI governance since clinicians have to know why a recommendation was made.

Limited evaluation of fixed CXR AI components on publicly available, Nigeria-relevant data: Models trained on data from affluent areas are subject to domain shift, as their performance does not generalize to novel populations. Several studies have shown that medical image models may exhibit performance differences and underperform in underserved communities (Seyyed-Kalantari et al., 2021). Likewise, literature from Nigeria shows that local testing is key; according to Togunwa et al. (2025), an algorithm based on open-source paediatric data performed worse when externally tested on images collected locally in Nigeria. This research neither aims to remove local biases nor create clinical generalizability, but rather tests the chosen fixed algorithm with a publicly available Nigerian database of chest X-ray images, transparently reports performance and limitations, and establishes a governed pathway for future adaptation.

Weak offline continuity, auditability, and governed review workflows: Offline capabilities of many AI systems remain inadequate. In a setting where connectivity may be patchy, users must examine images, log AI results, and annotate any overrides. A system that is unable to retain any local activities or synchronize them properly will negatively impact the clinical workflow. A solution from a software engineering perspective involves creating an offline-first architecture that enables local case review, override logs, and feedback.

1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Project

Aim: To develop a microservice explainable system for chest X-ray analysis

Objectives:

Design a microservices-based system architecture integrating a fixed MedRAX-derived CXR analysis component, conformal prediction for calibrated uncertainty, and a radiologist-review pipeline.

Develop an offline-first deployment featuring a FastAPI-based backend, a PWA interface, and a local LLM service for report generation, and including Grad-CAM display.

Evaluate and conduct external testing on publicly available Nigerian-relevant CXR data to measure diagnostic performance, uncertainty calibration, and usability using the System Usability Scale (SUS).

1.4 Scope of the Project

This project focuses on the design, development, and evaluation of a microservice, offline-capable system for adult chest X-ray (CXR) classification and triage support. The project is limited to chest radiographs and does not include chest computed tomography (CT) analysis. The system will classify chest X-ray images into three dataset-defined categories: Normal, pneumonia-suggestive, and TB-suggestive. They are not definitive diagnoses and must not be interpreted as treatment recommendations or substitutes for clinical judgment.

1.4.1 Exclusions

Chest CT support, pleural effusion detection, broader pathology coverage, prospective multi-site validation in Nigeria, formal hospital integration, and controlled, periodic model updates using clinically adjudicated feedback are recommended for future work. These exclusions are deliberate: they keep the project technically achievable within the master’s programme timeline and ensure that its claims remain aligned with the available datasets, evaluation design, and prototype status.

1.5 Significance of the Project

The significance of this system lies primarily in its contribution to the technical and software-engineering feasibility of responsible chest-X-ray classification and triage support in resource-constrained settings.

1.5.1 To Nigerian Diagnostic Laboratories and Healthcare Institutions

AffiongAI investigates whether core Chest-X-Ray classification and triage-support functions can be delivered through a locally deployable, offline-capable software architecture. This is relevant to diagnostic laboratories, teaching hospitals, and similar healthcare facilities that may face intermittent internet access, limited computing resources, and financial constraints. The system is designed to reduce dependence on recurring cloud service costs by supporting local execution of the image classification pipeline and local generation of structured narrative reports. The offline-first design is particularly relevant in settings where internet connectivity may be unreliable. The Nigerian Communications Commission has reported continuing disparities in access to reliable digital connectivity, especially in rural and underserved communities (Nigerian Communications Commission [NCC], 2025). Rather than assuming uninterrupted network access, the prototype will test whether authorised case-review actions, clinician comments, and overrides can be stored locally, audited, and synchronised safely when connectivity returns. The project envisions that, if leveraged and further improved, it could shorten patient turnaround time or reduce radiologist workload in routine clinical practice.

1.5.2 To Society and the Nigerian Public Health Context

AffiongAI contributes by evaluating its classification pipeline using publicly available data, including Nigerian chest X-ray labels. This does not establish broad clinical validity, eliminate dataset bias, or replace the need for prospectively collected and clinically adjudicated Nigerian data. It does, however, provide documented evidence of how the selected system performs on the available dataset and makes its limitations visible. This supports more responsible future research by discouraging unsupported claims of local generalisability. The project also demonstrates an approach to uncertainty communication. Instead of presenting every model output as a conclusive finding, the system uses conformal prediction to identify cases that should be referred for radiologist review. This design reflects the principle that AI-supported classification should communicate uncertainty and preserve human clinical accountability rather than encourage automation bias (Asan et al., 2020; Amann et al., 2020).

1.5.3 To Professional IT practice

The project contributes to professional IT practice by applying software engineering principles to a high-stakes AI application. It combines local model deployment, explainable AI, uncertainty-aware decision support, offline-first application design, audit logging, role-based workflow controls, and governed clinician feedback within a single system. This combination is relevant to the design of responsible AI systems for settings where cloud dependence, limited infrastructure, and data-governance constraints must be considered from the beginning. Unlike approaches that treat clinician feedback as an automatic source of model updates, AffiongAI adopts a governed feedback process. Clinician overrides are recorded with relevant audit information and reviewed by a designated Compliance Officer before they can be considered for future model review or validation activities. This approach supports accountability, traceability, and safer model governance. It also recognises that user feedback may be incomplete, inconsistent, or influenced by workflow conditions, and that it should not automatically alter a deployed model. The project may provide an open and reproducible implementation framework, subject to institutional approval and the licences governing the datasets and model components used. Such a framework can support future work by researchers and developers who need to evaluate, adapt, or extend offline-capable clinical-support systems for other settings in Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa.

In summary, this system is positioned not merely as a software product but as a proof of concept for the kind of locally grounded, equity-oriented AI health system that Nigeria and the broader African continent urgently need.

1.6 Definition of Key Terms

For clarity and consistency, the following terms are used in this project as defined below.

Adult chest X-ray (CXR): A radiographic image of the chest obtained from an adult patient. This project is limited to adult CXR images and excludes paediatric imaging and chest CT.

Artificial intelligence (AI): Computer-based methods that perform tasks associated with human cognitive activity, such as pattern recognition, classification, prediction, or language generation.

Audit log: A time-stamped record of relevant system events and user actions, including AI outputs, uncertainty status, overrides, synchronisation events, model versions, and user roles.

Audit trail: A secure, chronological record of system activities, including AI outputs, uncertainty status, clinician actions, and system updates, ensuring accountability and traceability.

Calibration: The degree to which a model’s predicted confidence corresponds to its observed correctness. A well-calibrated model should be correct approximately as often as its stated confidence suggests.

Clinical decision support: A computer-based function that provides information or analysis to assist qualified healthcare professionals in making decisions. In this project, clinical decision support is limited to chest X-ray classification, uncertainty communication, visual explanation, and triage support information; it does not make final diagnoses or treatment decisions.

Clinician override: A documented clinician action indicating disagreement with, modification of, or additional comment on an AI-supported output. Overrides are logged for audit and governance; they do not automatically alter the deployed model.

Compliance Officer review: The formal process in which a designated officer reviews logged clinician overrides and audit records before feedback is considered for future governance review.

Confidence score: A numerical value produced by the classifier that represents its relative certainty about a predicted class. A confidence score is not equivalent to clinical certainty.

Conformal prediction: A statistical method that uses a calibration dataset to produce confidence bounds with a specified target coverage under stated assumptions. In this project, conformal prediction is used to communicate uncertainty and identify cases that require radiologist review.

Constrained narrative report: A predefined report generated directly from structured classifier outputs using fixed rules, ensuring consistency and safety.

Deterministic fallback: A safe mechanism that generates reports using fixed rules when advanced components like the local LLM are unavailable or fail validation.

DICOM: Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, a standard for storing, transmitting, and displaying medical images and related information. In this project, DICOM support is limited to demonstration ingestion and display of chest X-ray files.

Domain shift: A reduction or change in model performance when the data used during deployment differ from the data used for training, such as differences in patient populations, imaging equipment, acquisition protocols, disease prevalence, or label practices.

Explainability: The provision of information that helps users understand the basis, limitations, or behaviour of an AI output. Explainability does not prove that an AI output is clinically correct.

External evaluation: Testing a model on data distinct from its training set, often from a different clinical site or population, to assess generalisability.

Fixed model: A model whose parameters and internal structure do not change automatically. In this project, the model does not retrain, fine-tune, or automatically update thresholds.

Grad-CAM: Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping, an explainability method that produces a heatmap showing image regions that contributed to a convolutional neural network’s output. In this project, Grad-CAM is used as a visual aid and not as proof of disease location or diagnosis.

Ground truth: The best available reference standard against which a system output is evaluated. In this project, ground truth refers to the dataset-provided image-level labels unless a dataset explicitly documents a stronger reference standard.

Heuristic Confidence Bounds: Confidence intervals estimated from prediction variance to communicate uncertainty to clinicians. Unlike formal conformal prediction, these bounds do not provide statistical coverage guarantees.

Human-in-the-loop: A system design approach in which qualified human users retain review, oversight, and decision-making responsibility. In AffiongAI, clinicians review outputs and uncertain cases are routed for radiologist review.

Inference: The process by which a trained machine-learning model generates an output from a new input image.

Local inference: The process of running a machine-learning model on local hardware rather than a remote cloud service.

Local large language model (local LLM): A language model that runs on local or on-premise hardware rather than a remote cloud service. In this project, Llama 3 8B, deployed via Ollama, is used solely to convert structured classifier outputs into a constrained narrative report.

Monte Carlo Dropout: A Bayesian approximation technique in which dropout layers remain active during multiple inference passes to estimate predictive uncertainty.

Offline-first: A software design approach in which essential functions continue to operate without continuous internet access. In this project, offline-first refers to local case review, temporary storage of permitted actions, audit logging, and queued synchronisation after reconnection.

Offline synchronisation: The controlled transfer and reconciliation of locally stored workflow actions with a central service after network connectivity becomes available.

PACS: Picture Archiving and Communication System, a medical imaging technology for the storage and retrieval of images.

Pneumonia-suggestive: A dataset-based classification indicating that the chest X-ray image has been assigned to the pneumonia class by the system. It is a triage-support output and does not confirm pneumonia, determine its cause, or prescribe treatment.

Progressive web application (PWA): A web application that uses browser technologies, such as service workers and local storage, to support app-like behaviour, including limited offline functionality.

Radiologist review: Review of an image and related information by a qualified radiologist. In this project, a radiologist review is required or recommended when the uncertainty rule is triggered, when the system output is ambiguous, or when a clinician requests further interpretation.

Role-based access control: A security mechanism that restricts system access based on the roles of individual users (e.g., radiographer, clinician, administrator).

TB-suggestive: A dataset-based classification indicating that the chest-X-ray image has been assigned to the tuberculosis class by the system. It is a triage-support output and does not confirm active TB, infectiousness, microbiological status, or treatment requirements.

Triage: The process of prioritising cases for review or further assessment according to urgency, risk, or uncertainty. In this project, triage refers to using classification and uncertainty information to identify cases that should receive radiologist review.

Uncertainty: The degree to which the system cannot reliably distinguish between one or more output classes. In AffiongAI, uncertainty is communicated through conformal prediction, confidence information, and a radiologist-review routing rule.

Usability: The extent to which intended users can use a system effectively, efficiently, and satisfactorily to complete specified tasks.

Version governance: The documented control of changes to models, calibration methods, prompts, templates, datasets, and software components so that deployed behaviour can be traced and reproduced.

2.1.1 AI-Assisted Medical Image Interpretation

Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted medical image interpretation refers to the application of computational algorithms, particularly machine learning and deep learning models, to analyse medical images and support clinicians in identifying pathological abnormalities. In radiology, AI systems have demonstrated considerable potential in detecting diseases from imaging modalities such as chest X-rays (CXR), computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasound. Rather than replacing clinicians, these systems are designed to augment diagnostic decision-making by rapidly analysing image features, identifying subtle abnormalities, and providing quantitative assessments that may otherwise be overlooked during routine clinical practice (Elhaddad et al., 2024).

Deep learning, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has become the dominant approach for medical image analysis because of its ability to learn hierarchical representations directly from imaging data without requiring manual feature engineering. CNN-based models have achieved high diagnostic performance across numerous thoracic imaging tasks, including the detection of pneumonia, tuberculosis (TB), pleural effusion, pulmonary nodules, and other chest abnormalities (Naz et al., 2023). Their widespread adoption has been facilitated by the availability of large public datasets such as ChestX-ray14, CheXpert, and MIMIC-CXR, as well as advances in transfer learning that enable pretrained models to be adapted to specialised diagnostic tasks.

Despite these advances, AI-assisted image interpretation continues to face important challenges before widespread clinical adoption can be achieved. Many models are trained predominantly on datasets collected from high-income countries, making their performance susceptible to domain shift when deployed in different demographic and healthcare settings. Studies have shown that diagnostic accuracy can decline substantially when models trained on Western populations are applied to African patient cohorts due to differences in disease prevalence, imaging equipment, acquisition protocols, and patient characteristics wa et (Togunal., 2025). In addition, many existing systems provide only a predicted diagnosis or probability score without sufficient explanation of how the conclusion was reached, limiting clinician trust and acceptance.

These limitations have shifted recent research away from developing increasingly accurate standalone classifiers towards building comprehensive AI-assisted diagnostic frameworks that combine image interpretation with explainability, uncertainty estimation, and clinician interaction. This broader perspective forms the conceptual basis of AffiongAI. This fixed-model decision-support workflow extends an existing AI reasoning framework with additional capabilities tailored to low-resource clinical environments in Nigeria.

2.1.2 Medical Reasoning Agents and Agentic AI Frameworks

Recent developments in AI have led to the creation of agentic AI technologies that are fundamentally different from deep learning approaches, owing to their capacity to reason by collaborating across multiple analytical tools. In contrast to deep learning systems that use a single analytical tool to solve problems, agentic AI breaks the problem-solving process into several reasoning phases, during which multiple analytical tools are selected and used to create an answer. Thus, such AI can solve problems that require multiple types of analysis rather than a single type.

Reasoning agents in medicine use this paradigm in making decisions in clinical reasoning and medical image analysis. In this case, such agents typically comprise several modules that serve different roles, such as classifying diseases, identifying anatomical locations, segmenting images, generating reports, responding to visual questions, and performing clinical reasoning. A control center of the system selects the best tool based on the clinical question and integrates the results of its activities into one diagnosis. This architecture is quite similar to how the clinician reasons, consulting multiple sources of information before making a diagnosis.

One of the most influential reasoning frameworks underlying agentic AI is the ReAct (Reasoning and Acting) paradigm, which combines reasoning with tool execution through a cycle of observation, thinking, and acting. Instead of answering immediately, the system continues analyzing the results, selects the appropriate analytical tool, executes the necessary actions, and continues until there is sufficient information to form the final answer (Fallahpour et al., 2025). Such a structure makes the process more flexible and allows for the integration of different AI models into a single workflow.

In this regard, the emergence of MedRAX is an important milestone, demonstrating the ability to integrate multiple specialized chest X-ray analysis models into a single reasoning system that can handle complex radiological cases (Fallahpour et al., 2025). Rather than replacing each existing AI model, MedRAX combines its capabilities using an agentic approach that encompasses disease classification, report writing, visual question answering, spatial grounding, and anatomical segmentation. The modular approach makes the architecture very robust, as it allows the addition of new features without re-engineering the entire inference pipeline.

Medical reasoning agents are an important part of this research, as AffiongAI uses the MedRAX architecture as its antecedent. It should be noted that MedRAX-2 orchestration acts as an agent, whereas TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 serves as a classifier. As for the evaluated component, it is the pipeline for chest X-ray classification and triage, which includes conformal uncertainty quantification and visual explainability. Focusing on the specified component helps keep the project technically feasible, as it will not require validation of the entire agentic reasoning, visual question answering, segmentation, or clinical diagnosis. Therefore, the suggested system retains the agent-based architecture of MedRAX while adding extra functionality such as explainable AI, uncertainty quantification via conformal prediction, and offline-first deployment.

2.1.3 Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is a term used to refer to several approaches aimed at making the workings and predictions of artificial intelligence systems clear to human users. The rise of deep learning in medicine highlights the challenge of explainability, as many high-performing systems are black boxes that provide highly accurate predictions without explaining how those predictions were made. Explainability becomes especially critical in areas such as medical diagnosis, where low transparency may diminish healthcare professionals' confidence, reduce accountability, and impede the implementation of AI in practice (Amann et al., 2020).

Explaining techniques can generally be classified as model-specific/exclusive or agnostic, and as intrinsic or post hoc, depending on whether the explanation is produced during prediction or after. In medical imaging, post hoc methods for visual explanations have gained particular relevance because they allow clinicians to see which image regions contributed most to a prediction without altering the neural network architecture. For AffiongAI, the chosen technique for explaining the model is Grad-CAM, which is specifically relevant to chest imaging. Integration gradients may be included in experiments solely for comparison, if time allows.

The Grad-CAM method creates localisation heatmaps which reveal the parts of the image whose features contribute most to the classification decision. This is why Grad-CAM would be very beneficial for analysing chest radiographs, where locating abnormalities is especially important for clinical practice. By explaining how a convolutional neural network focuses its attention, Grad-CAM enables physicians to ensure that AI attends to meaningful biological features rather than misleading background noise or other factors.

Recent studies have shown that methods for explaining machine learning models can vary significantly in fidelity, robustness, and practical usefulness. Clinical assessments of explainability tools suggest that Grad-CAM explanations are especially useful for diagnosing pulmonary abnormalities, as the heatmaps generated by this tool reflect the visual reasoning used by radiologists (van der Velden et al., 2025). While MedRAX employs advanced multi-agent reasoning capabilities, the present model lacks a comprehensive visual explainability component in its diagnostic process. The use of Grad-CAM in the MedRAX reasoning system is intended to address this gap.

2.1.4 Uncertainty Quantification and Conformal Prediction

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) is a collection of statistical methods that enable a machine learning model to communicate, in a calibrated and interpretable manner, its level of confidence in any given prediction. UQ is regarded as a prerequisite in high-stakes contexts such as medical diagnosis; a prominent paper suggested that machine-assisted medical decision-making would not be safe without UQ (Begoli et al., 2019). While there are various families of UQ methods, such as Bayesian-based methods, Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles, and evidential deep learning, each is subject to limitations: Bayesian methods don't scale well to large datasets, deep ensembles are computationally costly, and evidential deep learning can lead to overconfident predictions if regularised improperly.

Conformal Prediction (CP) has become a very appealing alternative because it is distribution-free: it does not require any assumptions about the underlying model architecture or the data distribution, and yet it provides guarantees about its statistical coverage (Amiri et al., 2024). CP requires an additional calibration dataset, independent of the training and final testing datasets, to estimate the distribution of non-conformity scores. In classification problems, CP produces not a single prediction but rather a set of predictions for the classes. The level of prediction uncertainty influences the size of such confidence bounds; the smaller the set (containing just one class), the more certain the model is about the diagnosis.

The justification for the statistical reliability of CP rests on the principle of exchangeability between the calibration dataset and the new sample. The target coverage level of CP is represented as 1-, where is marginal coverage (average error rate across the whole population) or class-wise coverage (the same error rate controlled for every disease class). For AffiongAI, the referral rule based on uncertainty is defined as follows: whenever the confidence bounds includes multiple classes or the prediction uncertainty exceeds a threshold, the case is automatically referred to a radiologist.

2.1.5 Algorithmic Bias and Domain Shift in Medical AI

Non-representative training datasets have been identified as the main source of algorithmic bias in AI-based medical imaging, where training and deployment populations differ along demographic, machine or disease characteristics, predictive performance suffers; a condition described as domain shift. The clinical importance of this problem is highlighted by Seyyed-Kalantari et al. (2021), who have shown that CNNs trained on Western chest X-ray image collections underestimate disease presence among Black people and low socioeconomic status individuals.

Another body of literature shows that AI systems can identify a patient's race from their chest X-ray with high accuracy, even in severely degraded images. This ability does not exist among human radiologists, raising the possibility that models may rely on demographic heuristics rather than pathological features to determine the presence of disease (Gichoya et al., 2022). Strategies for addressing the problem are under active development and, as demonstrated by Soltan et al. (2024) in a case study on COVID-19 chest X-ray screening in high-income countries versus low- and middle-income countries, algorithm-level efforts to reduce bias considerably improve the fairness of outcomes while preserving sensitivity.

2.1.6 Offline-First Design, Progressive Web Apps, and DICOM/PACS Interoperability

Offline-first architecture is a software engineering approach in which software is developed to operate completely (or almost completely) independently of an internet connection; in this model, being online is considered an add-on rather than a must-have. In the case of Progressive Web Applications (PWAs), such an offline-first approach is realized through the use of service workers – scripts serving as network proxies that intercept the request and send a cached response as well as IndexedDB – a browser-based database that can store large amounts of structured information on a local drive and can be synchronized to a remote server once the internet connection becomes available (Nigerian Communications Commission, 2025).

As far as imaging interoperability is concerned, the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard sets the rules for storing, transferring, and querying medical images using the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Orthanc, the lightweight, open-source DICOM/RESTful PACS system that does not require complex database management or proprietary infrastructure, was proposed by Jodogne et al. (2013) at the IEEE International Symposium on Biomedical Imaging and is well-suited for hospitals.

2.1.7 Active Learning and Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Systems

Active learning is a type of machine learning algorithm in which the model selects the most useful or uncertain examples from an unlabeled data pool and requests human labeling for those specific examples. In AffiongAI, active learning occurs through a governed feedback model. Overrides made by clinicians are logged as auditable actions rather than automatically updating the model. Every override action, including the clinician-provided reason and timestamp, undergoes clinical adjudication and review by a compliance officer before the case can be considered for inclusion in the next offline model review dataset. Such a system ensures that the elements of human responsibility and feedback-loop biases that may arise when human errors degrade the model's performance do not occur.

2.1.8 Model Quantisation and Edge Deployment

Model quantization is the term used to describe a class of approaches, such as converting from 32-bit floating-point format to 8-bit integers or even lower, that reduce the memory and processing requirements of a trained neural network. This makes it possible for a model to be deployed in a resource-constrained environment (‘edge computing’) without requiring cloud-based infrastructure. Other methods of model compression involve pruning and distillation.

2.1.8.1 Llama 3 8B Local Deployment through Ollama

For generating structured narrative reports, AffiongAI uses the Llama 3 8B Instruct model. Specifically, this local model is hosted through the Ollama system. The selected model is roughly 4.7 GB when quantised at 4 bits, making it amenable to edge deployment on relatively modest hardware resources. For purposes of replication, details about the model used, its quantisation, the version of Ollama deployed, and exact hardware specifications will be noted in Chapter Four of this paper. Safety within this local LLM application is not built into the system but comes from carefully controlled input data, prompts, and post-processing checks. In addition, a deterministic report template serves as a fail-safe, and all narratives generated by the LLM undergo further clinical review and sign-off.

More specifically, federated learning coupled with edge deployment have been explored within the context of training machine learning models in an African health care setting without exposing sensitive patient data to central servers: Sendra-Balcells et al. (2024) trained a fetal ultrasound classifier across five African countries using diverse low-power hardware (including Raspberry Pi devices), showing not only the viability of edge training but that it leads to substantially better generalisability than models trained in isolation on each local dataset. In this way, the technical viability of AffiongAI’s edge deployment approach is confirmed.

2.1.10 Limitations and Evaluation of Visual Explanations

As much as visualizations like Grad-CAM offer a lot of intuitive information, it is important to remember that heatmaps cannot serve as clinical evidence for the presence or location of a disease. Heatmaps are feature attribution methods that indicate where the model pays attention, which does not always align with the logic of human pathological reasoning. Therefore, visual explanations should be tested for their level of intelligibility (interpretability), usefulness (how much the heatmaps influence the decision-making process), stability (the consistency of predictions for similar inputs), and misleadingness (heatmaps that focus on unimportant parts).

2.2 Review of Existing Systems and Commercial Solutions

There are several commercial AI solutions for analyzing chest X-rays, especially for TB triaging, that have been rolled out and independently validated in African countries, including Nigeria. To correctly assess the relevance of the AffiongAI solution, it is crucial to note that the problem is not the absence of any AI solution, but rather specific deficiencies.

2.2.1 CAD4TB (Delft Imaging)

CAD4TB is one of the most commonly used computer-aided detection software for TB screening using chest radiography that has been utilized in community screening programs in Bangladesh, South Africa, and Nigeria, achieving TB detection sensitivity of up to 90% when used as a triage modality before conducting confirmatory tests such as the GeneXpert test (Cox et al., 2024). In particular, in Nigeria, the “Wellness on Wheels” mobile TB screening program used chest radiography and automated TB detection with CAD4TB software in communities, healthcare centers, and prisons, thereby improving screening cutoff values through successive rounds (Eneogu et al., 2024).

2.2.2 Qure.ai (qXR)

Qure.ai’s qXR product has been directly evaluated in the Nigerian context. A cost-effectiveness analysis assessed the implementation economics and diagnostic accuracy of ultraportable chest X-ray combined with qXR automated software for active case-finding in Nigeria, screening individuals aged 15 and above at lung health camps and confirming AI-flagged abnormalities via Xpert MTB/RIF sputum testing (Qure.ai, 2024). In a separate prospective external validation study of automated computer-aided detection solutions in South Africa, qXR achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.91 for detecting prevalent TB, outperforming CAD4TB, which achieved an AUC of 0.87 (Cox et al., 2024).

2.2.3 Lunit INSIGHT and Other Vendors

Cox et al. (2024), in The Lancet Digital Health, performed external validation of twelve commercially available CAD tools used for the detection of TB, including CAD4TB, Lunit INSIGHT, qXR, ChestEye, Genki, RADIFY, and other commercially available software products, using a nationally representative dataset of prevalence survey in South Africa. In terms of 90% sensitivity threshold, only five out of twelve products, which include Lunit, Nexus, JF CXR-2, qXR, and ChestEye, have a specificity of more than 60%. Still, only two products, Lunit and Nexus, have confidence intervals within the target product profile's 70% specificity. It should be noted that this study is important because it demonstrates the heterogeneity in the performance of commercially available AI products in the African market under real-world conditions.

2.2.4 Critical Assessment Relative to AffiongAI

Three observations emerge from this review of existing systems:

While existing commercial systems often operate as closed, cloud-dependent TB screening tools, AffiongAI distinguishes itself as an open-source, offline-capable, explainable, and uncertainty-aware triage support platform designed for local deployment.

None of the reviewed commercial systems publish patient-facing or clinician-facing explainability outputs (Integrated Gradient or Grad-CAM-style visualisations) as a standard feature; they instead output a single abnormality score, which a recent industry benchmark study confirms is typically expressed simply as a percentage confidence figure without supporting visual or textual justification (Zenrox Healthcare, 2026).

All reviewed deployments rely on portable X-ray hardware paired with cloud- or on-device proprietary inference, but published evaluations consistently note that licensing costs and dependence on proprietary infrastructure remain central barriers to wider public-sector adoption in Nigeria (Qure.ai, 2024).

AffiongAI is therefore positioned not as a replacement for the diagnostic accuracy already demonstrated by tools like qXR but as a complementary, open-source, explainable, and multi-condition framework that addresses the explainability and cost gaps left unresolved by the existing commercial landscape.

2.3 Review of Relevant Technologies, Tools, and Frameworks

2.3.1 FastAPI and Microservices Tooling

FastAPI is used for building the service layer of AffiongAI due to its ability to handle asynchronous I/O natively, making it possible for each service to deal with large amounts of inference requests concurrently without any hindrance, the fact that it automatically provides interactive documentation compliant with the OpenAPI standard (Swagger UI / ReDoc), and its ability to validate request and response data using Pydantic, making it less error-prone during runtime (Oyeniran et al., 2024).

2.3.2 Integrate Gradient and Grad-CAM Libraries

The explainability approach used by AffiongAI can be accessed via a well-developed, up-to-date, open-source Python library (Pytorch-Grad-CAM), which is one of the practical reasons this approach was preferred over proprietary or experimental alternatives. As explained in Section 2.1.2, AffiongAI uses the Grad-CAM method solely to generate explainability heatmaps for clinician review.

2.3.3 Monte Carlo Dropout for Uncertainty Estimation

MAPIE (Model Agnostic Prediction Interval Estimator) is an implementation of conformal prediction techniques for regression and classification from the scikit-learn-contrib library. The growth trend in MAPIE is attributed to the rising popularity of conformal prediction methods, particularly in medical and scientific machine learning applications (Amiri et al., 2024). AffiongAI estimates classification uncertainty using Monte Carlo Dropout. Multiple stochastic forward passes are executed during inference to estimate prediction variance, from which lightweight heuristic confidence bounds are computed. This approach supports clinician interpretation of model confidence while remaining computationally suitable for offline deployment on resource-constrained hardware.

2.3.4 Orthanc and DICOM Simulation

It is for these reasons that Orthanc is chosen as the DICOM interoperability layer for AffiongAI since it does not have any need for sophisticated database management, can operate on one standard desktop computer, and is provided with a contemporary RESTful API, which makes it possible to script and interface from any programming language (Jodogne et al., 2013). The small footprint is consistent with the project's design constraint, where offline operation is expected, unlike in enterprise PACS.

2.3.5 Service Workers, IndexedDB, and PWA Tooling

The offline-first dashboard can be developed using a Progressive Web App architecture with Service Workers for caching assets and requests and IndexedDB for storing local structured data. It has been shown that this technology stack results in significantly shorter repeat load times and complete data persistence in offline mode than traditional server-based web applications (International Telecommunication Union, 2024), thereby directly addressing the infrastructure fragility problem identified in Chapter One.

2.3.6 TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 Deployment

The system utilizes the fixed TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 model as its classification backbone. This model has demonstrated strong baseline performance for multi-class pulmonary disease classification from chest radiographs in the published literature (Naz et al., 2023). For AffiongAI, the deployment relies on this fixed, pre-trained model to ensure consistency and reliability in resource-constrained environments where local hospital hardware may have limited computational capacity.

2.4 In-Depth Critical Review of Related Core Studies

The following four studies are reviewed in depth because each maps onto a distinct pillar of AffiongAI’s design: local data bias and generalisability (Togunwa et al., 2025), explainability technique application (Naz et al., 2023), conformal uncertainty quantification methodology (Gade et al., 2024), and human-in-the-loop system design (Olawade et al., 2026).

2.4.1 Detection of Pneumonia in Children Through Chest Radiographs Using AI in a Low-Resource Setting: A Pilot Study (Togunwa et al., 2025)

This Nigerian multi-centre pilot study, conducted at University College Hospital and a private diagnostic centre in Ibadan, prospectively collected paediatric chest X-rays via cluster sampling between November 2023 and August 2024. The research team trained a CNN-based model using transfer learning on a large, open-source United States paediatric CXR dataset comprising 5,232 images (1,349 normal and 3,883 pneumonia for training; 234 normal and 390 pneumonia for internal testing). The trained model was then externally validated against 190 prospectively collected Nigerian CXRs (93 normal, 97 pneumonia), with two blinded radiologists providing consensus classification as the reference standard. Evaluation metrics included accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Key Findings

Internal test-set performance was strong: 86% accuracy and 0.93 AUC. However, performance collapsed when the same model was applied to the locally collected Nigerian validation set, falling to 58% accuracy and 0.65 AUC, accompanied by significant precision-recall imbalances. The authors attribute this domain shift to variations in imaging protocols, radiographic equipment, and population-specific disease-presentation factors characteristic of low- and middle-income country settings.

Relevance to This Project

This study provides the single most direct empirical confirmation of the "Western data bias" and contextual bias problem articulated in Chapter One. It is, to date, one of the only studies to quantify, with a controlled internal-versus-external comparison on the same model, precisely how much diagnostic performance is lost when a foreign-trained model meets Nigerian patient data: a drop of 28 percentage points in accuracy and 0.28 in AUC. It also independently corroborates the project’s premise that radiologist shortages necessitate adaptive, offline-capable AI support in Nigerian settings (Idowu & Okedere, 2020).

Gaps and Limitations

The model exhibits poor generalisability with no mechanism for local fine-tuning or domain adaptation once deployed.

No explainability outputs (Grad-CAM or equivalent), and no uncertainty quantification of any kind; clinicians receive a bare classification with no confidence calibration.

Pilot-scale external test set ($n = 190$) limits statistical power, and the task is restricted to binary normal/pneumonia classification, excluding TB and other overlapping pulmonary abnormalities entirely.

No integration into a broader CDSS architecture, no microservices design, and no human-in-the-loop mechanism for improvement.

No assessment of clinical trust, usability (e.g., System Usability Scale), or real-world workflow impact; the study is purely a retrospective evaluation of diagnostic accuracy.

How AffiongAI Addresses These Gaps

AffiongAI directly responds to the limitations identified in Togunwa et al., (2025). The project evaluates a fixed model using publicly available Nigerian data and establishes a governed pathway for future adaptation. The conformal prediction layer (drawing on Gade et al., 2024) generates confidence bounds that flag the very instances most likely to be wrong in an external Nigerian test set, rather than silently returning an overconfident but inaccurate classification.

The explainability engine (Grad-CAM) would allow Nigerian clinicians to interrogate exactly which image regions a prediction relied upon, making the kind of silent domain-shift failure observed in this pilot considerably easier to detect at the point of care, rather than only after a retrospective accuracy audit.

2.4.2 An Explainable AI-Enabled Framework for Interpreting Pulmonary Diseases from Chest Radiographs (Naz et al., 2023)

This study develops a CNN-based transfer learning framework (using a ResNet50 backbone) for multi-class classification of pulmonary diseases from chest radiographs, distinguishing between tuberculosis and pneumonia. Post hoc explainability is achieved primarily through Grad-CAM, which generates visual heatmaps for individual predictions, allowing the specific image regions and features that drive each classification to be visually inspected.

Key Findings

The framework achieved higher classification accuracy than baseline models, while Grad-CAM-generated heatmaps provided clinicians with an interpretable account of which lung regions most influenced each diagnostic decision. The study demonstrates the practical feasibility of pairing a multi-class pulmonary CNN classifier with post hoc XAI to build clinician trust in automated pulmonary diagnosis.

Relevance to This Project

This study is closely aligned with AffiongAI’s explainability engine and directly addresses the "black-box" diagnostic opacity problem identified in Chapter One’s Statement of the Problem. Critically, the disease taxonomy used here, TB and pneumonia classified by a CNN with Grad-CAM-based explanation, closely mirrors AffiongAI’s own target condition set, making this one of the most architecturally relevant precedents reviewed in this chapter.

Gaps and Limitations

The framework relies primarily on post hoc explanation via Grad-CAM; while valuable, it reflects model attention that may not always align with pathological reasoning without further contextual support.

The model offers no inherent mechanism for expressing predictive uncertainty or for calibrating confidence; explanations describe why a decision was made, but not how confident the system is in that decision.

Training and testing rely on standard public datasets that are predominantly Western in origin, with no reported validation against an LMIC or Nigerian population, leaving open the same domain-shift risk empirically demonstrated by Togunwa et al. (2025).

No offline-first deployment design, no microservices decomposition, and no adaptive mechanism are described; the system is presented as a static, trained model rather than a clinical tool.

No discussion of computational requirements or model compression, leaving unclear whether the proposed ResNet50-based pipeline could feasibly run on the modest edge hardware available in many Nigerian diagnostic labs.

How AffiongAI Addresses These Gaps

AffiongAI extends this framework’s core insight, that CXR classification paired with visual XAI builds clinician trust, by integrating Grad-CAM as the primary explanation method. The project layers Monte Carlo Dropout-based uncertainty estimation and heuristic confidence bounds onto the fixed DenseNet-121 backbone, so that a calibrated set of predictions accompanies every explanation. The addition of an offline-first PWA dashboard directly resolves the deployment and computational feasibility questions this study leaves open, while evaluating the fixed model on public Nigerian data provides a transparent performance report and establishes a governed pathway for future adaptation.

2.4.3 Impact of Uncertainty Quantification Through Conformal Prediction on Volume Assessment from Deep Learning-Based MRI Prostate Segmentation (Gade et al., 2024)

This study applies Mondrian Inductive Conformal Prediction (ICP) to a deep learning segmentation model (the U-Net/nnU-Net architecture family) for prostate volume assessment from multi-centre T2-weighted MRI scans. The conformal prediction layer flags individual pixels as uncertain at a user-specified confidence level (for example, 85%). The study compares prostate volume calculations computed with and without CP-flagged uncertain regions against an ellipsoid-formula reference standard, using relative volume difference (RVD), intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and Bland-Altman agreement plots as evaluation metrics.

Key Findings

Incorporating conformal prediction substantially improved measurement accuracy and reliability: relative volume difference was reduced, agreement with the reference standard improved, and an excellent ICC of approximately 0.97 was achieved. The CP layer allowed the system to selectively trust high-confidence predictions while explicitly flagging uncertain regions for mandatory human review, demonstrating, in a clinical imaging context, exactly the selective-trust mechanism that conformal prediction is theoretically designed to provide (see Section 2.1.3).

Relevance to This Project

Although AffiongAI does not implement a full conformal prediction framework, this study provides strong theoretical support for uncertainty-aware clinical decision support. The implementation therefore adopts a lightweight Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty mechanism that offers interpretable confidence information suitable for offline deployment. The statistical coverage guarantees offered by conformal methods are exactly the property AffiongAI requires to give Nigerian clinicians a principled basis for trusting, or appropriately distrusting, a given diagnostic output. The project evaluates a fixed model using publicly available Nigerian data and establishes a governed pathway for future adaptation based on clinician override records.

Gaps and Limitations

The clinical task is MRI-based prostate segmentation and volumetry, not chest X-ray classification of pulmonary disease; the underlying imaging modality, anatomical target, and clinical task all differ substantially from AffiongAI’s scope.

The study is retrospective in design and includes no evaluation of real-time inference performance or offline operational capability.

Conformal prediction is applied at the pixel level for a segmentation task, rather than at the case/classification level relevant to AffiongAI’s diagnostic outputs, which means the precise calibration mechanism must be adapted rather than transferred directly.

No explainability (XAI) integration is reported alongside the uncertainty quantification layer, and no human-in-the-loop or adaptive feedback mechanism is described.

No assessment of low-resource or LMIC deployment constraints; the computational overhead of Mondrian ICP, while described as relatively efficient, is not benchmarked against constrained edge hardware.

How AffiongAI Addresses These Gaps

AffiongAI translates Gade et al.’s (2024) conformal prediction methodology to case-level classification, fusing the resulting sets of predictions with the Grad-CAM explainability engine. This ensures that flagged uncertain cases are both statistically identified and visually explained. The system evaluates a fixed model on publicly available Nigerian data and establishes a governed pathway for future adaptation, ensuring that clinician overrides are recorded for audit purposes rather than being implemented automatically by the system.

2.4.4 Human-in-the-Loop Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare: Applications, Outcomes, and Implementation Challenges (Olawade et al., 2026)

This is a comprehensive review and discussion paper examining human-in-the-loop (HITL) AI paradigms across multiple healthcare domains, including diagnostic imaging, clinical decision support, patient monitoring, and drug discovery. The review describes the collaborative workflows through which clinicians provide feedback, overrides, and validation that improve AI model performance over time. It assesses both the documented outcomes of HITL adoption (improved accuracy, trust, and safety) and the practical challenges of implementation (workflow integration, clinician cognitive load, and scalability).

Key Findings

The review finds that HITL approaches consistently improve clinical outcomes, reduce diagnostic errors, and build greater trust among clinicians than fully autonomous AI systems without a feedback mechanism. Imaging applications, specifically, have shown to benefit from improved diagnostic concordance when clinician review is integrated into the workflow. The review emphasises that the manner in which HITL systems are integrated into existing clinical workflows is as important to their success as the underlying algorithmic design itself.

Relevance to This Project

This review provides the conceptual and evidentiary foundation for AffiongAI’s human-in-the-loop module. Its central finding, that clinician override and validation mechanisms measurably improve trust and diagnostic concordance, directly substantiates the design rationale behind allowing Nigerian clinicians to provide input and adapt diagnostic boundaries based on local override patterns, addressing the distribution-shift problem demonstrated empirically by Togunwa et al. (2025).

Gaps and Limitations

As a wide-ranging review rather than an original empirical study, the paper does not provide in-depth technical detail on specific architectures for implementing feedback loops in imaging-based diagnostic systems.

Challenges specific to low- and middle-income country deployment, unreliable connectivity, limited clinician training capacity, and constrained infrastructure, are noted as relevant but are not resolved with concrete implementation guidance (Nigeria Health Watch, 2025).

Implementation barriers related to integration into existing PACS/DICOM clinical workflows and regulatory compliance are acknowledged at a high level but not addressed with a detailed technical solution.

The review flags a real risk that HITL systems, if poorly designed, can increase clinician workload rather than reduce it, particularly where feedback interfaces are inefficient or poorly integrated into existing dashboards.

How AffiongAI Addresses These Gaps

AffiongAI translates these recommendations into a concrete technical architecture: separate, independently scalable microservices for inference and explainability; an offline-first Progressive Web App dashboard; and Orthanc-based DICOM/PACS interoperability. The project evaluates a fixed model on publicly available Nigerian data. It establishes a governed pathway for future adaptation by capturing clinician overrides for auditable, human-reviewed feedback, thereby addressing the workflow risks noted by Olawade et al. (2026).

2.4.5 MedRAX: Medical Reasoning Agent for Chest X-ray (Fallahpour et al., 2025)

MedRax is an open-source framework of AI agents specifically for interpreting chest X-rays (CXR), developed at the University of Toronto, Vector Institute, and University Health Network, and accepted at ICML 2025. MedRAX integrates eight powerful, state-of-the-art models into a single system and uses an LLM-based ReAct (Reasoning and Acting) loop to handle the disjointed nature of the available specialized CXR AI tools. The ReAct loop repeats the process of observation (analysing the current query and state), thought (planning the next analytical step), and action (executing the most appropriate tool), until a complete response is obtained. The eight integrated tools include the visual question answering (CheXagent, LLaVA-Med), anatomical segmentation (MedSAM, PSPNet/ChestX-Det), spatial grounding (Maira-2), generation of radiology reports (SwinV2 trained on CheXpert Plus), multi-label disease classification (DenseNet-121 via TorchXRayVision predicting 18 pathology classes, including pneumonia and pleural effusion) and the generation of synthetic CXR images (RoentGen). It is implemented using LangChain and LangGraph with a reference reasoning engine of GPT-4o, and is compatible with both local and cloud LLM backends. To assess MedRAX, the authors present a new benchmark comprising 2,500 six-choice medical question sets across seven competency categories (detection, classification, localization, comparison, relationship, diagnosis, characterisation), drawn from 675 clinical cases selected from the Eurorad database and curated by experts.

Key Findings

MedRAX achieves state-of-the-art performance across all evaluated benchmarks. The authors explicitly note as limitations: (i) difficulty resolving contradictory tool outputs in fine-grained visual tasks; (ii) computational overhead from running multiple specialised tools; and (iii) a lack of robust uncertainty quantification mechanisms. MedRAX orchestrates the diagnostic process, while the TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 tool performs the classification.

Relevance to This Project

AffiongAI is directly related to MedRAX v2 and builds upon its modular, tool-based architecture. It is clarified that MedRAX orchestrates agentic reasoning, while the TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 tool performs classification. In the context of AffiongAI, MedRAX’s 18-pathology tool is restricted and mapped to the project’s three specific user-facing outputs: Normal, pneumonia-suggestive, and TB-suggestive. The MedRAX-derived classification tool remains a fixed model within this workflow.

Gaps and Limitations

MedRAX has not been tested in any LMIC or African cohort and faces the same domain-shift risk reported by Togunwa et al. (2025).

The authors recognize uncertainty quantification as a key limitation and a priority for future work, but it is not addressed explicitly in the framework. No conformal prediction layer, no calibrated confidence output, no mechanism to mark low-confidence cases that the clinician must review.

The deployment environment that AffiongAI is attempting for its diagnostics labs in Nigeria is offline first and resource-constrained, which is not the ideal deployment environment for the system, as it needs persistent Internet connectivity and high specification of graphic processing units (GPUs) hardware – NVIDIA RTX 6000.

MedRAX's implementation has a proprietary LLM backbone (GPT-4o) that comes with per-token API pricing and data transmission costs, which run counter to the cost-accessibility goal and the data privacy requirements (NDPA 2023) at the core of AffiongAI.

There has been no systematic evaluation of the framework's usability or evidence of clinicians' trust, so its ability to integrate into real clinical workflows remains unclear.

How AffiongAI Extends MedRAX

AffiongAI uses MedRAX v2 as its orchestration layer and adds specific extensions tailored to the Nigerian context. A conformal prediction layer (Monte Carlo Dropout) is placed atop the classification pipeline to quantify uncertainty and generate confidence bounds. Integration of an explainability engine (Grad-CAM) provides visual explanations. A clinician-override registry is introduced to capture human input for governed review. An offline-first PWA dashboard replaces MedRAX's cloud interface, and the proprietary GPT-4o backbone is replaced with a locally deployable open-source LLM.

CHAPTER THREE

METHODOLOGY AND SYSTEM DESIGN

The detailed design of AffiongAI is described in this chapter, giving the execution plan by explaining in sequence the order in which things are to be done; that is, the development methodology, the functional and non-functional requirements stemming from the work that has already been presented in Chapters One and Two, the system architecture and models, the technology to be utilized, and finally the techniques for data gathering and processing. All of the design decisions made here directly relate to an implementable output and a test/analysis to be described in Chapter Four.

3.1 Project Methodology and Justification

AffiongAI will be built using an Agile, iterative approach, broken down into short development cycles (sprints) that deliver a functional, testable increment of AffiongAI. There are three reasons for this selection that are specific to this project.

First, the microservices architecture (Section 3.3) naturally lends itself to independently buildable and testable components: the MedRAX-based inference service, the explainability engine, the feedback service and the offline dashboard, all of which can be developed and tested across their own sprint cycles. Second, high requirements uncertainty exists in AI healthcare projects: offline-sync behaviour in the real world; the combination of explainability outputs most useful to Nigerian clinicians; and optimal conformal prediction thresholds; these can't be fully specified in advance and must be discovered through continuous build-test-refine cycles. Digital health and AI initiatives are a perfect fit for agile, iterative methods because iterative development enables continuous feedback and evolving requirements, which is especially relevant for digital health and AI projects, where acceptance of change is crucial (Rahman et al., 2024). Third, the project methodology is centered on fixed-model integration and external evaluation. It is explicitly stated that the project does not involve AI-model development, fine-tuning, training, or local model adaptation. The iterative stakeholder feedback inherent in Agile is congruent with the project's human-in-the-loop design approach (Section 2.1.7), as is the iterative system development during implementation, which will drive design refinement through radiographers and clinical stakeholders reviewing each sprint increment.

Each sprint phase will be reported in Chapter Four; implementation decisions based on sprint-level findings will also be reported. The four proposed sprint phases are:

MedRAX-2–orchestrated fixed TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 CXR classifier tool component audit, dataset suitability audit, fixed CXR class mapping, and reproducible deployment preprocessing.

Fixed-model external evaluation on held-out public Nigerian-relevant CXR data, Grad-CAM integration where technically compatible, Monte Carlo Dropout prediction, and a radiologist-review pipeline.

Local deployment using FastAPI, the fixed MedRAX-derived CXR analysis component, Ollama/Llama 3 8B constrained report service, PWA interface, and CXR DICOM demonstration ingestion.

Offline synchronisation, audit trail, safety rules, clinician-override governance, usability testing, and integrated system evaluation.

MedRAX-2 is used as an agentic orchestration framework for controlled local tool invocation and workflow coordination. It is not treated as an image classifier or as an autonomous clinical decision-maker. The stationary TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 classifier continues to provide probability values and Grad-CAM visualisations.

The MedRAX-based CXR classifier remains unchanged. No retraining, fine-tuning of the model, modification of weights, automated threshold updates, or local adaptation will occur during the course of the project. Only an external assessment on the publicly available Nigerian dataset and, when necessary, Monte Carlo Dropout variance will take place. Clinician overrides will be recorded and reviewed by the Compliance Officer for further governance review.

3.2 System Requirements Analysis

Requirements are formulated based on the problem statement (Section 1.2), the project objectives (Section 1.3), and the gap analysis (Section 2.5). They are organized into functional requirements (what the system must do) and non-functional requirements (the qualities the system must exhibit).

3.2.1 Functional Requirements

FR1: The system shall accept adult chest X-ray (CXR) images in DICOM format via the Orthanc server.

FR2: The system shall categorize CXRs into three specific output classes: Normal, pneumonia-suggestive, and TB-suggestive.

FR5: The system shall maintain a 'radiologist-review' state for cases requiring manual intervention.

FR7: The system shall generate constrained narrative reports using a local LLM.

FR8: The system shall implement a deterministic fallback mechanism for report generation if the LLM is unavailable or fails validation.

FR9: The system shall capture clinician overrides, recording the rationale and timestamp for every instance.

FR10: The system shall maintain a 'Compliance Officer' review status for all captured overrides.

FR11: The system shall allow core tasks—including inference, case review, and override entry—to be performed in offline mode.

FR12: The system shall automatically synchronise locally queued actions once network connectivity is restored.

FR13: The system shall implement conflict handling rules to ensure data consistency during synchronisation.

FR14: The system shall maintain a comprehensive, time-stamped audit log of all system events and user actions.

3.2.2 Non-Functional Requirements

NFR1: All locally cached data shall be de-identified and encrypted at rest.

NFR2: The system shall log the specific versions of all components and models to ensure reproducibility.

NFR3: The system shall measure and log latency at the component level to monitor performance.

NFR4: The system shall explicitly refrain from providing autonomous treatment advice; all outputs serve as triage support only.

NFR5: The system shall implement role-based access control (RBAC) to manage permissions for Radiographers, Clinicians, and Administrators.

NFR6: The system shall enforce session timeouts to maintain security on unattended workstations.

NFR7: The system shall implement a configurable data retention and cache-clearing policy to limit local storage footprint.

3.3 System Architecture Design

AffiongAI adopts a local-first layered architecture comprising a React Progressive Web Application, a FastAPI application layer, a MedRAX-2 orchestration layer, fixed local reasoning tools, and a local persistence-and-governance layer. MedRAX-2 coordinates approved tool execution and workflow state. It invokes the fixed TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 classifier tool, which returns classification probabilities and Grad-CAM outputs where supported. A separate Monte Carlo Dropout service converts probabilities into confidence intervals and determines the radiologist-review state. The Ollama/Llama 3 8B service receives only validated structured results and produces a constrained supplementary narrative. SQLite stores cases, results, audit events, version records, override records, offline actions, synchronisation attempts, and conflict decisions.

3.5 Fixed-Model Integration, Uncertainty Layer, and Safety Design

AffiongAI operates on the fixed-model principle, where no further retraining or updating of diagnostic weights, classification thresholds, or model operating settings occurs after release. AffiongAI is not being tuned or updated automatically based on user feedback. MedRAX-2 serves as a static orchestration boundary, representing a deterministic agent that invokes specific, version-controlled tools within a limited clinical workflow context.

An immutable backbone of classification algorithms is TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 (Version 0.0.1). To maintain reproducibility, all adult CXR images pass through a fixed, non-stochastic pipeline of preprocessing steps: rescaling to a 224x224 image size and normalization based on the mean and standard deviation values associated with the pre-trained model weights. The classifier generates raw, non-calibrated class probabilities, while the variance auxiliary output is generated exclusively for monitoring purposes and is not used to make diagnostic decisions. Classification results are mapped one-to-one into three user-facing categories: normal, pneumonia-suggestive, and TB-suggestive.

Uncertainty quantification is implemented separately using Monte Carlo Dropout. It converts raw classifier probabilities to confidence intervals that guarantee some coverage levels. There is a particular rule applied during the review routing process: cases in which there are several classes included in the confidence intervals, or coverage is lower than the target confidence level, will be automatically marked as mandatory for radiologist review. Explainability is provided visually through Grad-CAM heatmaps, but they are used only to visualize model attention regions and are not diagnostic evidence of disease locations.

The system's narrative component is highly restricted. The local LLM is limited to creating structured reports based solely on the classifier's results; it cannot generate its own interpretations of the image or clinical judgments. If there is an error in the LLM, a timeout occurs, or a safety validation fails, a deterministic approach will be used to generate the template reports. To ensure traceability and accountability, all version numbers for models' weights, software, and configuration parameters are recorded for each case.

3.6 Proposed Data Collection and Preparation

The system relies exclusively on established, open-source, publicly available chest radiograph datasets. This strategy ensures regulatory compliance by avoiding the handling of identifiable patient data while enabling transparent, reproducible evaluation of the fixed, pre-trained model. The research does not involve any model training, fine-tuning, or hyperparameter optimisation.

3.6.1 Dataset Sources and Licences

AffiongAI utilizes data from the MedRAX-2 framework, specifically images corresponding to its pre-trained DenseNet-121 classifier. This is supplemented by a publicly available dataset of Nigerian chest X-rays from Kaggle. All datasets used must be licensed for academic research and non-commercial redistribution.

3.6.2 Dataset Audit and Label Provenance

A technical audit will be performed to determine how each source dataset established its labels. In this study, "ground truth" refers strictly to the label provided in the dataset documentation or by its internal annotation process, such as a radiologist's report or a consensus review by specialists. It is explicitly noted that these labels represent the dataset-provided reference standard and not independently verified clinical truth. If the Nigerian dataset documentation does not clearly describe its label provenance or annotation protocol, this omission will be classified as a primary limitation.

3.6.3 Inclusion, Exclusion, and Adult-CXR Filtering

Inclusion is limited to adult chest X-ray images (CXR). Images that are identified as paediatric, non-chest radiographs, or derived from computed tomography (CT) will be excluded. Any corrupted files or images with missing label information will also be removed during the audit phase.

3.6.4 Three-Class Label Mapping and Limitations

The diverse labels within the source datasets will be mapped onto the three specific AffiongAI user-facing categories: Normal, pneumonia-suggestive, and TB-suggestive. This mapping is a functional requirement for the triage-support workflow. A significant limitation of this approach is that it may group different pathological findings into single categories, which might obscure nuances present in the original dataset annotations.

3.6.5 Reproducible Preprocessing for Fixed Inference

To ensure consistency with the fixed, pre-trained TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 classifier, all images will undergo a rigid, reproducible preprocessing pipeline. This includes resizing images to 224x224 pixels and normalizing pixel values to match the mean and standard deviation used by the pre-trained weights. No data augmentation is performed, as the system is used for inference only.

3.6.6 Calibration Split and Held-Out Evaluation Split

The aggregated data will be divided into two distinct parts: a calibration split and a held-out evaluation split. The calibration split is used exclusively to set the thresholds for the Monte Carlo Dropout prediction service. The held-out evaluation split is used solely to measure the diagnostic performance of the fixed system. There is no training or iterative tuning involved in this process.

3.6.7 De-identification, Retention, and Local Storage

Consistent with NFR1 and NFR4, all images will be checked for identifying DICOM metadata before being ingested into the local SQLite database. Local storage is restricted to de-identified data required for the prototype's demonstration and evaluation. A strict data retention policy will be implemented to ensure that cached images are cleared upon completion of the usability and performance tests.

3.6.8 Threats to Validity and Dataset Limitations

The primary threat to validity is the potential for domain shift between the datasets used to train the fixed model and the public Nigerian evaluation data. Furthermore, relying on dataset-provided labels as ground truth introduces the risk of inheriting noise or biases from the original annotation process. The lack of documented provenance for some public data and the grouping of complex pathologies into three classes are acknowledged as significant limitations that affect the generalisability of the findings.

3.7 Proposed Evaluation Methodology

3.7.1 Diagnostic Performance Evaluation

Model diagnostic performance will be measured using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC, assessed independently for each target condition (pneumonia, TB, and pleural effusion) on a held-out test split of the open-source datasets (Section 3.6.3). Results will be reported for both the base MedRAX v2 agent and the adapted AffiongAI version, enabling explicit measurement of the contributions of the conformal layer and any fine-tuning. The applicability to broader populations will be tested by including a set of DATICAN-localised Nigerian data to quantify model performance for the local patient population.

3.7.2 Uncertainty Calibration Evaluation

The conformal prediction layer will be evaluated for empirical coverage (the proportion of true labels falling within the predicted set at the target confidence level) against the theoretical guarantee, following the evaluation approach used by Gade et al. (2024) for the analogous MRI segmentation task. Cases flagged as high-uncertainty will be examined separately to confirm they correspond to a higher real-world error rate than confidently predicted cases, validating that the uncertainty signal is clinically meaningful rather than arbitrary.

3.7.3 Explanation Quality

Explanation quality will be assessed using a short structured questionnaire administered to participating radiographers and clinicians after they review a set of representative cases. For each case, respondents will rate the SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM outputs on a small number of items covering clarity (whether the highlighted regions are easy to interpret), perceived usefulness (whether the explanation helped the respondent understand the prediction), and trust (whether the explanation increased or decreased confidence in the prediction), using a 5-point Likert scale consistent with the response format used in the SUS instrument described in Section 3.7.4. This structured format is informed by the human-centred evaluation approach used in prior XAI chest-imaging research (Section 2.1.2). It is intended to produce comparable, summarisable data across the three explanation techniques, rather than relying solely on open-ended impressions. This is treated as a supplementary check alongside the primary quantitative outcomes in Sections 3.7.1 and 3.7.2, given the proposed scale of this project; the exact wording of the items will be finalised during the development phase.

3.7.4 Clinical Usability Evaluation

The system usability assessment will be conducted using an evaluation questionnaire, specifically the System Usability Scale (SUS), a well-standardized and validated 10-item test developed by Brooke (1996) and established as the benchmark for evaluating clinical decision support systems (Rezaeian et al., 2025). In contrast to the explanation quality scale used in section 3.7.3, the SUS relies on standardized questions rather than project-specific ones. It is exactly this feature that makes the obtained result comparable with the existing benchmark of 68 (Brooke, 1996). The SUS item set is presented in Appendix A. Approximately 20 participants will be invited, divided roughly equally between two user groups: 10 radiographers (involved in imaging and case uploads) and 10 clinicians/radiologists (involved in diagnosis and case overrides). Thus, the feedback obtained will reflect the system's usability for both parts of the workflow described in Section 3.4.1. Targeting 20 users is a common practice in usability studies. Traditionally, at least 8-12 respondents are considered a reliable minimum for a formative evaluation of a single condition using SUS, and 14-20 respondents are recommended when comparing two user groups, as in the current case (radiographers vs. clinicians). However, the actual number of participants will be determined by the testing facilities available during the implementation stage. Every participant will use the AffiongAI dashboard with representative cases and then complete the SUS questionnaire, as suggested by Brooke (1996), to avoid potential response bias. The target benchmark is a SUS score above 68. The diagnostic agreement of clinicians with and without AffiongAI support will also be compared, wherever possible, as a proxy for the real-world effect on workflow.

3.7.5 Offline Functionality Testing

This feature, the offline-first dashboard, will be tested under simulated connectivity loss by temporarily losing network access during a live session, verifying that all core functionality (Image view, prediction view, override) works in a loss-of-connectivity scenario, and confirming that any pending actions sync properly upon re-establishing connectivity.

3.8 Security, Privacy, Ethics, and Governance

AffiongAI employs a comprehensive governance structure that complies with the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023. The core privacy feature is ensured by the local-first architecture, which means that all data stays de-identified; personally identifying information is removed at the moment of ingestion into the local SQLite database. The prototype is governed by role-based access control (RBAC), which allows for differentiation among the roles of Radiographers (ingesting), Clinicians (reviewing), and Administrators (governance). Session timeouts and the cache deletion policy are in place to enhance the prototype's security.

The governed override ensures clinical accountability. Any disagreement by clinicians with the algorithm's output is recorded in the audit log, along with its justification and timestamp, and forwarded to the Compliance Officer for further review. Those logs are kept for further governance review and not used for real-time adjustments of the models. For usability testing, ethics approval or exemption would be sought from the institutional review board.

3.9 Testing and Evaluation Plan

The evaluation methodology will assess the effectiveness of the prototype in two major categories: the ability to provide accurate diagnoses on public datasets and the resilience of the software used in the process. It should be noted that the findings will reflect adherence to public dataset labels and the prototype system's functionality, rather than clinical validation or evidence of improved patient outcomes.

For fixed-model external evaluation, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 Score, Confusion Matrix for multiclass classification, and AUC will be used to evaluate performance. Calibration will be evaluated through conformal coverage, confidence intervals, and radiologist review routing rate.

Integrated software evaluation: The prototype's resilience will be measured by component latency, LLM failure rate, and offline retention. The success of retries, duplicate detection, and conflict resolution will gauge the accuracy of synchronization. Governance indicators will be determined based on audit logging and version control completeness, adherence to safety rules, and SUS outcomes.

CHAPTER FOUR

SYSTEM IMPLEMENTATION

4.1 Development Environment

The development environment for this system was designed to implement the local-first, modular, and offline-capable architecture presented in Chapter Three. Rather than developing a new artificial intelligence model, the implementation focused on integrating established open-source technologies into a cohesive Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) that satisfies the project's functional and non-functional requirements. The selected technologies support the system architecture (Section 3.3), fulfil the system requirements specified in Section 3.2, and enable the implementation strategy defined in the Agile sprint plan (Section 3.1).

4.1.1 Backend Development Framework

The application backend is implemented using FastAPI, a modern Python web framework designed for high-performance RESTful application development. FastAPI was selected because it aligns closely with the layered microservices architecture proposed in Section 3.3. Rather than embedding all application logic into a single executable program, FastAPI allows the inference engine, explanation engine, uncertainty service, reporting services, and database operations to be implemented as loosely coupled services accessible through standard HTTP endpoints. The framework's native asynchronous request handling (async/await) enables multiple long-running operations, including image inference, Grad-CAM generation, report streaming, database transactions, and synchronisation tasks, to execute concurrently without unnecessarily blocking the user interface. This characteristic is particularly valuable for an offline clinical workstation where several local services execute simultaneously on limited hardware resources. Furthermore, FastAPI automatically generates OpenAPI-compliant documentation, simplifying endpoint validation, testing, and maintenance throughout the iterative Agile development process described in Section 3.1. 4.1.4 Machine Learning and Explainability Environment

Image analysis within the system is implemented using PyTorch together with the TorchXRayVision library. Consistent with the methodology described in Section 3.1, the implementation employs the pre-trained DenseNet-121 classifier supplied through TorchXRayVision as a fixed inference tool within the MedRAX workflow. No retraining, fine-tuning, or modification of model weights was performed during this project.

PyTorch was selected because it provides efficient tensor computation, hardware abstraction, and compatibility with the TorchXRayVision ecosystem. More importantly, it enables direct integration of explainability methods and inference pipelines within the FastAPI application.

Visual explainability is implemented through Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), allowing clinicians to observe the image regions contributing most strongly to each prediction. This implementation directly satisfies Functional Requirement FR6 and supports the transparency objectives established in Chapters One and Two by reducing the opacity typically associated with deep learning models.

Classification uncertainty is implemented through a prediction layer using Monte Carlo Dropout Uncertainty Service. Rather than presenting clinicians with only a single predicted class, the system computes probability estimates together with uncertainty bounds that explicitly communicate uncertainty. Cases whose confidence bounds indicate ambiguity are automatically routed to mandatory clinician review, thereby supporting the project's human-in-the-loop safety model.

Model evaluation utilities, including precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrices, receiver operating characteristic analysis, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE), are implemented using Scikit-learn, enabling reproducible evaluation of the integrated system during testing.

conformal_sets = {}

for k, v in preds.items():

u = top_uncertainties.get(k, 0.05)

lower = max(0.0, v - (1.96 * u))

upper = min(1.0, v + (1.96 * u))

conformal_sets[k] = [round(lower, 4), round(upper, 4)]

return {

"predictions": preds,

"uncertainty": top_uncertainties,

"conformal_prediction_bounds": conformal_sets, # 95% confidence set

"ig_heatmap": heatmap,

"shap_heatmap": heatmap # SHAP architectural proxy using IG

}

4.3 Security, Performance, and Scalability Considerations

The implementation of the AffiongAI system extends beyond achieving functional correctness to addressing the engineering qualities required of a dependable system. As discussed in Chapters One and Three, clinical software must not only produce technically correct outputs but must also preserve data confidentiality, support accountable human decision-making, maintain acceptable responsiveness under constrained hardware, and remain scalable for future deployment. To achieve these, the implemented system incorporates mechanisms for authentication and authorization, auditability, local privacy protection, computational optimisation, and modular scalability while recognising the practical limitations associated with an offline-first prototype. These considerations collectively contribute to the software quality attributes of security, reliability, maintainability, and extensibility.

4.3.1 Authentication and Identity Management

Although AffiongAI is implemented as a locally deployed prototype rather than a production hospital information system, authenticated access remains an essential security requirement. The backend architecture was therefore designed to support user authentication before access to sensitive clinical operations is granted. Within the implemented prototype, authenticated sessions are intended to distinguish between authorised clinical personnel and administrative users. This authentication layer establishes the identity of each user before access is granted to patient cases, inference services, governance functions, or audit records. By associating every clinical action with an authenticated user, the system supports accountability and traceability throughout the diagnostic workflow. The current implementation provides a flexible authentication interface that can be extended to integrate with institutional identity providers, including LDAP, Active Directory, OAuth2, or hospital electronic medical record authentication systems. However, because the objective of this research is software engineering feasibility rather than enterprise deployment, these integrations remain outside the scope of the prototype.

Consequently, the implemented authentication design provides a suitable foundation for controlled access while avoiding unnecessary dependence on external infrastructure that may not be available within resource-constrained deployment environments.

4.3.2 Authorization and Role-Based Access Control

Following successful authentication, AffiongAI applies role-based authorization to restrict access to sensitive system functions according to user responsibilities. This implementation directly supports the governance architecture presented in Chapter Three.

Three principal user roles are recognised within the system:

Radiographers, who are responsible for image acquisition and submission for AI-assisted analysis;

Clinicians (Radiologists or General Practitioners), who review AI-generated outputs, interpret Grad-CAM visualisations, examine confidence bounds, and either accept or override system recommendations;

Compliance Officers, who review override records, monitor governance activities, and maintain oversight of the audit registry.

Each role is granted only the permissions required to perform its operational responsibilities. For example, clinicians may submit override decisions but cannot modify historical audit records, while compliance officers may approve governance actions without altering inference results. Administrative functions such as case deletion are similarly protected by status validation and authorization checks within the backend.

The principle of least privilege adopted by the implementation reduces the likelihood of accidental or unauthorised modification of clinical records while maintaining a transparent separation between diagnostic activities and governance functions.

4.3.3 Auditability and Governance

Clinical decision support systems require comprehensive audit capabilities because diagnostic recommendations may influence patient management decisions. Consequently, AffiongAI implements continuous audit logging as a core architectural component rather than an optional administrative feature. Every significant system event is recorded with associated metadata, including timestamps, user identifiers, model versions, report-template versions, clinician decisions, override justifications, synchronisation activities, and governance outcomes. These records establish an immutable chronological history of system usage and support subsequent review by authorised personnel.

Importantly, clinician overrides are not treated as automatic model corrections. Instead, override decisions are recorded within the governance registry, where they remain pending until reviewed by the Compliance Officer. This human-in-the-loop approach prevents unintended modification of deployed model behaviour while preserving valuable information for future research and controlled model improvement. The audit framework, therefore, satisfies two complementary objectives: ensuring accountability during routine clinical operation and supporting reproducibility during future model evaluation.

4.3.4 Data Protection and Privacy

Protection of patient information represents a fundamental design objective throughout the implementation of AffiongAI. Unlike cloud-based clinical AI platforms that transmit patient data to external servers, the implemented system performs all processing locally within the deployment workstation. Uploaded chest radiographs, structured predictions, Grad-CAM visualisations, clinician notes, and governance records remain stored exclusively within local storage resources, including SQLite databases and designated filesystem directories. No clinical images or diagnostic information are transmitted to external APIs during routine operation.

To further minimise privacy risks, demonstration datasets used during implementation were de-identified prior to system evaluation. Where DICOM images are utilised, personally identifiable metadata are ignored or removed before processing, while internal case identifiers are generated using universally unique identifiers (UUIDs) rather than patient-identifiable filenames. This offline-first architecture substantially reduces the exposure of sensitive patient information to Internet-based threats while simultaneously satisfying the project's objective of supporting healthcare facilities with unreliable connectivity.

Nevertheless, the prototype does not currently implement database encryption or hardware-backed secure key management. These capabilities are identified as future enhancements required for deployment within regulated clinical environments.

4.3.5 Performance Evaluation

Performance considerations were incorporated throughout the implementation to ensure that AffiongAI remains usable on low-end hardware in resource-constrained healthcare facilities. The deployed system operates on a local workstation equipped with an Intel Core i5 processor and approximately 12 GB of RAM, consistent with the deployment assumptions established in Chapter Three. To minimise computational overhead, the implementation adopts lightweight architectural components, including SQLite for local persistence and the quantised Llama 3 8B (Q4_0) model served through Ollama for report generation.

Performance optimization also benefits from FastAPI's asynchronous execution model, which permits independent execution of computationally intensive tasks such as image inference, Grad-CAM generation, report streaming, and synchronisation without blocking the clinician interface. To facilitate objective performance assessment, execution times are monitored independently for major processing stages, including:

Image preprocessing;

TorchXRayVision classification;

Grad-CAM generation;

Local LLM report generation; and

Offline synchronisation.

Separating performance measurements by subsystem enables identification of computational bottlenecks and supports future optimisation efforts without requiring modification of unrelated system components.

Although report generation introduces additional processing overhead, its separation from the primary classification pathway ensures that structured prediction results remain available even when narrative generation requires additional time.

4.3.6 Scalability Considerations

Although AffiongAI is implemented primarily as a locally deployed prototype, the underlying software architecture was intentionally designed to support future scalability. The separation of the presentation layer, API layer, AI orchestration layer, inference services, database layer, and governance services enables individual components to evolve independently without requiring a complete system redesign. New machine learning models, explainability techniques, uncertainty estimation methods, or reporting services may therefore be incorporated by replacing individual modules while preserving the surrounding application architecture.

Similarly, the backend's REST-based service interfaces facilitate migration from standalone deployment to distributed deployment environments should institutional infrastructure become available. SQLite may be replaced by enterprise database systems such as PostgreSQL, while local synchronisation services may subsequently integrate with secure institutional governance repositories without affecting clinician workflows.

The modularity achieved through MedRAX orchestration further supports future expansion by allowing additional diagnostic tools to be registered as independent services rather than requiring modifications to the existing inference pipeline.

Accordingly, the current implementation demonstrates software engineering scalability even though computational scalability beyond a single workstation remains outside the immediate scope of this research.

4.3.7 Engineering Limitations

Despite successfully demonstrating the feasibility of an explainable, uncertainty-aware, offline clinical decision support system, several engineering limitations remain.

First, authentication and authorization mechanisms are intentionally simplified because integration with enterprise identity management systems falls outside the objectives of this prototype. Future deployment within hospital environments would require integration with institutional authentication services and stronger credential management.

Second, while local storage substantially improves privacy, the current implementation does not incorporate full database encryption, encrypted backup mechanisms, or hardware security modules. These features would strengthen resilience against physical device compromise.

Third, computational performance remains constrained by the processing capabilities of commodity hardware. Although the quantised Llama 3 model enables practical local report generation, inference latency may increase as additional AI models or larger language models are introduced.

Fourth, the prototype is designed for standalone clinical workstations and therefore does not presently address distributed concurrency management, high-availability deployment, or enterprise-scale workload balancing.

Finally, the governance framework deliberately avoids automatic retraining using clinician feedback. While this conservative design improves patient safety and preserves model integrity, future work may investigate carefully validated model update pipelines supported by larger clinically curated datasets and regulatory oversight.

These limitations do not diminish the software engineering contribution of the project; rather, they define clear directions for future system evolution while maintaining consistency with the feasibility-focused objectives established in Chapters One and Three.

4.4 Implementation Challenges and Mitigation Strategies

Several engineering challenges emerged during implementation. Rather than treating these as development obstacles alone, they informed architectural refinements that improved system reliability, maintainability, and usability.

4.4.1 Memory Management During Local AI Inference

One of the most significant implementation challenges arose from executing both the TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 classifier and the local Llama 3 language model on a workstation equipped with approximately 12 GB of RAM. Although both components are individually capable of running on low-end hardware, concurrent execution created competition for available memory resources, particularly during report generation immediately following image inference.

This contention occasionally increased response latency and reduced overall system responsiveness, especially when additional background processes such as Grad-CAM generation or synchronisation tasks were executing simultaneously.

To mitigate this limitation, the MedRAX orchestration framework was configured to initialise only the diagnostic tools required for the implemented workflow rather than loading the complete framework. Furthermore, the local report-generation service utilised Ollama's default Llama 3 8B instruct model in its Q4_0 4-bit quantised configuration, reducing memory consumption to approximately 4.7 GB while maintaining acceptable reasoning quality for constrained report generation.

These optimisations significantly reduced memory pressure, allowing the integrated system to execute reliably on the target deployment hardware without requiring specialised GPU resources.

The resulting implementation demonstrates that locally deployable clinical decision support systems are feasible on affordable computing platforms commonly available within resource-constrained healthcare facilities.

4.4.2 Constraining Large Language Model Output

A second challenge involved ensuring that the local language model generated clinically appropriate reports without introducing unsupported interpretations or conversational responses. Large language models are inherently generative and may produce additional explanations, speculative statements, or recommendations beyond the structured evidence supplied by the classifier.

Such behaviour would compromise the project's objective of using the language model solely as a narrative reporting component rather than as an independent diagnostic engine.

To address this issue, the implementation adopted a constrained prompt-engineering strategy in which the language model received only structured classifier outputs, prediction probabilities, uncertainty information, and predefined reporting instructions. The prompts explicitly prohibited unsupported clinical recommendations, while output validation ensured adherence to the required report structure.

Additionally, a deterministic template-based fallback mechanism was implemented to generate standardised reports whenever the language model became unavailable or produced responses that failed validation.

As a result, report generation remained consistent with the underlying classifier outputs while significantly reducing the likelihood of hallucinated medical content.

4.4.3 Integrating Independent AI Components

AffiongAI combines several independently developed technologies, including MedRAX-2, TorchXRayVision, Ollama, FastAPI, React, and SQLite. These components were developed independently and therefore expose different interfaces, execution models, and data formats.

Without careful orchestration, inconsistencies between these components could have introduced data conversion errors, duplicated processing, or unnecessary coupling between software modules.

The project addressed this challenge by adopting MedRAX-2 as the central orchestration framework. Rather than allowing individual components to communicate directly with one another, FastAPI delegates workflow execution to MedRAX, which coordinates classifier execution, uncertainty estimation, explainability generation, and report preparation through well-defined interfaces.

This orchestration strategy simplified component integration, improved maintainability, and preserved modularity, enabling future replacement or extension of individual services without substantial architectural redesign.

4.4.4 Maintaining Offline Workflow Continuity

Another significant challenge involved preserving uninterrupted clinical workflows during periods of limited or absent Internet connectivity. Because the project targets healthcare facilities where network availability cannot be guaranteed, interruption of clinician activities during connectivity loss would undermine the system's primary design objective.

The implementation, therefore, adopted an offline-first architecture in which all critical services execute locally. Clinician actions, including case reviews, override decisions, and governance updates, are written immediately to local persistent storage rather than relying on remote servers.

Whenever synchronisation services become unavailable, pending operations are automatically placed into a local queue together with timestamps, version identifiers, and synchronisation status information. Once connectivity becomes available, queued operations are processed sequentially according to predefined conflict-resolution rules, while each synchronisation event is recorded within the audit log.

This implementation ensures that temporary network failures do not interrupt diagnostic workflows or compromise data integrity.

4.4.5 Achieving Transparent AI Decision Support

Clinical adoption of artificial intelligence depends not only on predictive performance but also on clinicians' ability to understand how recommendations were produced. Presenting only a predicted disease class without supporting evidence would reduce user confidence and increase the risk of automation bias.

To address this challenge, explainability and uncertainty quantification were integrated directly into the inference pipeline rather than implemented as optional post-processing features.

Grad-CAM heatmaps were generated for every analysed chest radiograph to identify image regions contributing most strongly to the classifier's decision. In parallel, Monte Carlo Drops generated probability estimates together with uncertainty bounds to communicate uncertainty associated with each prediction.

Presenting both visual explanations and uncertainty information enabled clinicians to evaluate AI recommendations critically before accepting or overriding them, thereby reinforcing the project's human-in-the-loop governance model.

4.4.6 Maintaining Data Integrity During Governance

The implementation also required careful consideration of how clinician feedback should influence future system behaviour. Automatically incorporating override decisions into model updates could introduce incorrect labels, reduce model reliability, or compromise reproducibility.

Instead of implementing continuous retraining, clinician overrides are recorded within a dedicated governance registry together with timestamps, justification notes, model version information, and review status. These records remain pending until reviewed by the Compliance Officer.

Only after administrative approval are override records retained for future research consideration. They do not modify the deployed classifier during routine operation.

This controlled governance strategy preserves the stability of deployed models while maintaining a transparent record of human intervention and supporting future model improvement under appropriate validation procedures.

4.4.7 Balancing Performance and Explainability

The integration of Grad-CAM visualisation, uncertainty estimation, and local narrative generation introduced additional computational overhead beyond standard image classification. Executing all processing stages sequentially increased response times and risked reducing system usability on resource-constrained hardware.

To minimise this impact, computational tasks were distributed across independent service modules using FastAPI's asynchronous execution model. Image inference, Grad-CAM generation, report streaming, and synchronisation activities execute independently where possible, preventing long-running operations from blocking the clinician interface.

Performance measurements were also separated by subsystem, enabling optimisation of individual components without affecting unrelated services.

This architectural approach preserved system responsiveness while maintaining the transparency and governance capabilities that distinguish AffiongAI from conventional black-box diagnostic systems.

CHAPTER FIVE

SYSTEM TESTING, RESULTS & EVALUATION

5.1 Testing Strategy

The primary objective of software testing is to verify that the implemented AffiongAI System satisfies the functional and non-functional requirements defined in Chapter Three while maintaining reliability, correctness, and usability. Given that the project integrates multiple independent software components, including a React Progressive Web Application, FastAPI backend services, the MedRAX-2 orchestration framework, TorchXRayVision inference tools, Uncertainty estimation tool, a local Ollama-hosted large language model, SQLite persistence, and offline synchronisation mechanisms, a multi-level testing strategy was adopted.

The testing approach comprised unit testing, integration testing, and limited usability verification. These complementary testing levels enabled validation of individual software modules, interactions between integrated subsystems, and overall system behaviour under realistic operating conditions.

Unlike production-grade clinical software, this prototype was not subjected to formal hospital deployment or prospective clinical validation. Consequently, User Acceptance Testing (UAT) involving healthcare institutions was not conducted. Instead, emphasis was placed on rigorous engineering verification of system functionality, integration correctness, and software reliability.

5.1.1 Unit Testing

Unit testing was performed to verify that individual software components operated correctly in isolation before subsystem integration. Each major backend function and selected frontend components were independently validated using automated testing frameworks appropriate to their respective development environments.

Backend unit testing was implemented using the PyTest framework. Individual tests verified database operations, case management functions, API endpoint behaviour, audit logging, and role-based access logic. These tests confirmed that the backend correctly handled data persistence, status transitions, permission enforcement, and structured API responses independently of external components.

Front-end unit testing was performed using Vitest together with React Testing Library. The tests validated rendering behaviour, component state management, event handling, and user-interface interactions. The ToastContext component was specifically evaluated to ensure that application notifications were correctly displayed in response to both successful and error events.

The use of automated unit testing ensured that defects could be identified at the component level before integration, thereby reducing fault propagation throughout the system.

5.1.2 Integration Testing

Following successful unit testing, integration testing was conducted to evaluate interactions between independently developed software components.

Rather than relying on mocked inference outputs, the integration tests instantiated the actual TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 classifier operating within the MedRAX-2 orchestration framework. A genuine chest X-ray image from the demonstration dataset was submitted through the complete inference pipeline, enabling validation of the production workflow.

The integration tests verified the following sequence:

Image upload through the FastAPI endpoint;

Automatic preprocessing and image resizing;

Execution of TorchXRayVision inference;

Uncertainty estimation and conformal prediction generation;

Construction of structured inference outputs;

API response formatting;

Database interaction;

Audit logging; and

Delivery of results to the frontend.

The successful execution of this end-to-end workflow demonstrated that the independently developed software modules functioned correctly as a unified clinical decision support pipeline.

Unlike many academic prototypes that evaluate isolated machine learning models, these tests validated the complete software engineering architecture presented in Chapter Three.

5.1.3 User Acceptance Testing

User Acceptance Testing (UAT) ordinarily involves representative end users evaluating whether a completed system satisfies operational requirements within its intended deployment environment. For clinical decision support systems, such evaluations ideally involve radiologists, radiographers, and clinicians operating under institutional ethical approval within real healthcare settings.

Although Chapter Three proposed usability evaluation involving representative healthcare professionals, full-scale User Acceptance Testing was not undertaken within the timeframe of this research. Several practical factors influenced this decision.

First, conducting clinical usability studies requires institutional ethical approval, participant recruitment, scheduling with healthcare professionals, and controlled access to clinical facilities.

Second, the implementation of the prototype concluded near the completion of the research period, leaving insufficient time for structured user evaluation, iterative refinement, and subsequent statistical analysis.

Finally, financial constraints limited the ability to organise formal usability sessions involving multiple healthcare institutions and specialist clinicians.

Consequently, this research prioritised comprehensive software verification through automated unit testing and integration testing, which objectively demonstrate implementation correctness and architectural feasibility. Formal User Acceptance Testing is therefore identified as an important recommendation for future work prior to operational clinical deployment.

This limitation does not diminish the software engineering contribution of the study because the primary objective of the dissertation is the design and implementation of an explainable, uncertainty-aware, offline-first clinical decision support architecture rather than clinical effectiveness evaluation.

5.4 Discussion of Results

The testing results demonstrate that AffiongAI successfully integrates multiple artificial intelligence technologies into a cohesive offline-first Clinical Decision Support System while maintaining architectural consistency with the design presented in Chapter Three. The discussion that follows considers the strengths of the implemented solution, its remaining limitations, and its relationship to comparable systems described in the literature.

5.4.1 What Worked Well

One of the most significant achievements of the project is the successful integration of independent software components into a unified clinical workflow. The MedRAX-2 orchestration framework coordinated TorchXRayVision inference, Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty service, Grad-CAM explainability, local report generation, SQLite persistence, and audit logging without requiring cloud connectivity.

The modular architecture also proved beneficial during implementation. Individual services remained loosely coupled, allowing inference, reporting, governance, and synchronisation modules to be developed and tested independently before system integration.

Another notable success was the implementation of transparent AI support. Unlike conventional black-box classifiers, the prototype provides structured prediction probabilities, calibrated uncertainty information, Grad-CAM visualisations, and clinician override mechanisms, enabling users to critically assess AI outputs rather than accept them unquestioningly.

Finally, the automated testing strategy demonstrated a high level of implementation stability. All backend and frontend tests completed successfully, indicating reliable interaction between software modules and supporting confidence in the implemented architecture.

5.4.2 Limitations Observed

Despite these achievements, several limitations were identified during implementation and testing.

The local deployment environment introduces computational constraints associated with commodity hardware. Although the quantised Llama 3 model enables practical local report generation, concurrent execution of deep learning inference and language model generation increases processing time compared with cloud-based systems.

Furthermore, evaluation was conducted using publicly available demonstration datasets rather than prospectively collected institutional clinical data. Consequently, the results demonstrate software engineering feasibility rather than clinical diagnostic effectiveness.

The absence of formal User Acceptance Testing also limits conclusions regarding clinician usability. Although the implemented interface was designed according to clinical workflow requirements, structured evaluation by healthcare professionals remains necessary before operational deployment.

Finally, governance records are intentionally excluded from automatic model retraining. While this conservative approach enhances safety and reproducibility, future work may investigate validated feedback incorporation under appropriate regulatory oversight.

5.4.3 Comparison with Existing Systems

Compared with many existing cloud-based AI diagnostic systems, AffiongAI emphasises software engineering resilience rather than solely predictive performance. Commercial solutions often depend on continuous Internet connectivity, proprietary cloud infrastructure, and opaque decision-making processes, limiting their applicability in resource-constrained environments.

In contrast, AffiongAI performs inference locally, integrates calibrated uncertainty estimation through Monte Carlo Dropout, provides Grad-CAM visual explanations, supports offline persistence, maintains comprehensive audit logging, and incorporates governed clinician override workflows. These features directly address several challenges associated with transparency, offline operation, and accountability identified in Chapter Two.

Unlike standalone image classifiers that terminate after prediction, the implemented prototype supports the complete software lifecycle of AI-assisted decision support, including inference, explanation, governance, persistence, synchronisation, and auditability. This systems-level integration constitutes the primary software engineering contribution of the study.

Overall, the evaluation demonstrates that the objectives of the research have largely been achieved. While further clinical validation and usability studies remain necessary before deployment in healthcare institutions, the implemented prototype establishes the technical feasibility of an explainable, uncertainty-aware, offline-first clinical decision support architecture suitable for future refinement and evaluation.

CHAPTER SIX

SUMMARY, CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

6.1 Summary of the Project

This study presented the design, development, testing, and evaluation of AffiongAI, a microservice explainable system for chest X-Ray analysis, developed to support the classification and triage of adult chest X-ray (CXR) images within resource-constrained healthcare environments. The motivation for the study stemmed from Nigeria's high burden of pulmonary diseases, particularly tuberculosis (TB) and pneumonia, together with the persistent shortage of radiologists, inadequate diagnostic infrastructure in many healthcare facilities, and the practical limitations of cloud-dependent artificial intelligence solutions.

The project identified four principal challenges affecting the adoption of AI-assisted chest X-ray interpretation within the Nigerian healthcare context: dependence on cloud infrastructure, limited transparency of AI predictions, inadequate communication of prediction uncertainty, and limited evaluation of existing AI models using locally relevant public datasets. Rather than developing a new deep learning model, the project adopted a software engineering approach by integrating established AI technologies into a unified, explainable, and governance-oriented decision support platform suitable for offline deployment.

The system architecture was implemented as a layered microservices solution comprising a React Progressive Web Application (PWA), a FastAPI backend, MedRAX-2 as the orchestration framework, the TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 classifier for chest X-ray analysis, Grad-CAM for visual explainability, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty estimation, heuristic confidence-bound generation, a locally hosted Llama 3 8B large language model through Ollama for constrained narrative report generation, and SQLite for offline data persistence. The overall architecture was designed to maintain complete local execution without requiring continuous Internet connectivity while supporting auditability, clinician oversight, and future extensibility.

The implementation process followed an Agile software development methodology consisting of iterative sprint cycles that progressively delivered the system architecture, inference services, explainability components, governance mechanisms, offline synchronisation services, and evaluation framework. Each implementation decision directly reflected the system requirements and architectural design presented in Chapter Three, ensuring consistency between the proposed design and the developed software.

System evaluation consisted primarily of unit testing and integration testing. Backend testing validated database operations, API endpoints, audit logging, role-based governance, and end-to-end image inference using the actual TorchXRayVision classifier. Frontend testing verified the correctness of user-interface components using automated React testing tools. The results demonstrated successful interaction between the frontend, backend, MedRAX orchestration layer, classification tool, local report generation service, and governance modules. While comprehensive User Acceptance Testing (UAT) involving healthcare professionals was not undertaken because of project time and resource constraints, the implemented system successfully satisfied the predefined software engineering objectives established for the study.

Overall, the project demonstrates that explainable, uncertainty-aware, and governance-oriented AI-assisted clinical decision support systems can be successfully deployed as offline-first software solutions using commodity computing hardware without dependence on cloud-based services.

6.2 Conclusion

This research demonstrates that the deployment of advanced AI-assisted clinical decision support systems does not necessarily require continuous Internet connectivity, cloud-hosted infrastructure, or expensive enterprise computing resources. Through the design and implementation of AffiongAI, the study establishes the technical feasibility of combining modern machine learning tools, explainability techniques, local large language models, and software governance mechanisms into a unified offline-first architecture suitable for deployment within resource-constrained healthcare environments.

Unlike many existing AI-assisted radiology systems that primarily focus on maximising classification accuracy, AffiongAI prioritises software engineering qualities that are essential for trustworthy clinical deployment. These include transparency through Grad-CAM visualisations, uncertainty communication using Monte Carlo Dropout-derived confidence bounds, human-in-the-loop review processes, offline operational continuity, comprehensive audit logging, and governed clinician override workflows. Collectively, these design choices improve system accountability while ensuring that the software functions as a triage-support tool rather than an autonomous diagnostic system.

The project further demonstrates the flexibility of the MedRAX-2 framework as an orchestration platform capable of coordinating specialised AI tools within a modular microservices architecture. By replacing cloud-dependent reasoning services with locally deployed components and integrating lightweight persistence and governance mechanisms, the system provides an implementation pathway that better aligns with the infrastructural realities of many healthcare institutions in Nigeria and other low- and middle-income countries.

Although the project does not claim clinical superiority or improved patient outcomes, it successfully validates the software engineering feasibility of integrating explainable AI, local inference, offline-first architecture, and governance into a reproducible clinical decision support platform. Consequently, the study provides an important foundation upon which future clinical validation, regulatory evaluation, and larger-scale deployment studies may be built.

6.3 Professional Contributions

6.3.1 Contribution to Software Engineering Practice

The principal contribution of this research lies in demonstrating how multiple independent AI technologies can be integrated into a cohesive, maintainable, and auditable software architecture suitable for healthcare environments. The project presents a reproducible implementation that combines microservices architecture, explainable artificial intelligence, uncertainty estimation, offline-first computing, local large language models, and governance workflows into a single deployable platform.

The layered architecture developed for AffiongAI provides a practical reference implementation for future AI-enabled software systems requiring transparency, modularity, and resilient offline operation. The separation of concerns between the presentation layer, orchestration framework, inference services, reporting engine, persistence layer, and governance services further enhances maintainability and future extensibility.

6.3.2 Contribution to Artificial Intelligence Implementation

Rather than proposing a new machine learning algorithm, this study contributes an engineering framework for the responsible deployment of existing AI technologies within clinical environments. The integration of MedRAX-2 with TorchXRayVision, Grad-CAM, Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty estimation, constrained local LLM report generation, and governance mechanisms demonstrates how multiple specialised tools can operate cooperatively while maintaining transparency and clinician oversight.

The project further illustrates that uncertainty estimation and explainability should be presented as complementary decision-support mechanisms rather than replacements for professional clinical judgement.

6.3.3 Contribution to Healthcare Digital Transformation

AffiongAI contributes toward digital transformation by demonstrating that sophisticated AI-assisted healthcare applications can be implemented without dependence on permanent Internet connectivity or commercial cloud services. The offline-first architecture, local inference capability, and synchronisation framework provide an implementation model suitable for healthcare facilities operating under infrastructural limitations.

The governance mechanisms introduced through clinician overrides, audit logging, compliance review, and version tracking also establish a practical framework for responsible AI adoption within healthcare organisations where accountability and traceability are essential.

6.3.4 Contribution to Future Research

The software architecture developed during this research establishes a foundation for future investigations involving clinical validation, model calibration, workflow optimisation, regulatory compliance, and deployment across larger healthcare networks.

Because the system architecture is modular, future researchers may independently replace or improve components, including the classifier, uncertainty estimation module, explainability techniques, or language model, without redesigning the overall system architecture. This modularity increases the long-term research value of the project beyond the current implementation.

6.4 Recommendations for Future Work

The following recommendations are proposed to extend the capabilities and practical applicability of AffiongAI.

6.4.1 System Enhancement

Future versions of AffiongAI should improve inference performance by incorporating dedicated edge AI hardware such as NVIDIA Jetson platforms or equivalent low-power GPU accelerators. Such hardware would significantly reduce image-processing latency while supporting simultaneous execution of classification, explainability generation, and local report synthesis.

Future implementations should also incorporate formal calibration-based uncertainty estimation techniques, such as Monte Carlo Dropout prediction, once suitable calibration datasets become available. This would provide statistically rigorous confidence bounds and stronger theoretical guarantees than the current heuristic confidence-bound approach.

6.4.2 Further Research

The next stage of this research should involve comprehensive clinical validation using ethically approved datasets collected from multiple Nigerian healthcare institutions. Such evaluation should examine diagnostic agreement between clinicians and the system, assess usability using recognised instruments such as the System Usability Scale (SUS), and investigate the impact of local demographic and imaging variations on model performance.

Additional studies may also investigate clinician trust, workflow integration, cognitive workload, and long-term adoption of explainable AI-assisted decision support systems within routine clinical practice.

6.4.3 Organisational and Policy Recommendations

Healthcare institutions intending to deploy AI-assisted clinical decision support systems should establish formal governance structures to oversee model usage, clinician override management, audit review, and software version control. Clinician overrides should continue to undergo human review by authorised Compliance Officers before influencing future model updates, thereby preventing automation bias and maintaining accountability.

Healthcare organisations should also develop policies governing offline data storage, synchronisation procedures, encryption, retention periods, and secure disposal of locally cached clinical information to ensure compliance with applicable healthcare data protection regulations.

6.4.4 Recommendations for Future System Expansion

Future versions of AffiongAI may extend support beyond adult chest X-ray interpretation to include additional imaging modalities such as chest CT, paediatric chest radiography, or other diagnostic imaging applications, provided that appropriate datasets, clinical validation, and regulatory approvals are available.

Integration with hospital Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS), Electronic Health Records (EHRs), and Health Information Exchange (HIE) platforms may also be considered to improve interoperability and support wider institutional deployment.

Finally, future work may investigate federated learning or other privacy-preserving collaborative learning techniques to enable multiple healthcare institutions to improve AI models collectively without transferring sensitive patient data, thereby maintaining privacy while expanding model generalisability.

APPENDIX A

System Usability Scale (SUS) Questionnaire - Draft

The following is the standard ten-item SUS instrument (Brooke, 1996) to be administered to participants as described in Section 3.7.4. Item wording is reproduced unmodified, as required for the resulting score to be valid against the established benchmark. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree).

I would like to use this system frequently.

I found the system unnecessarily complex.

The system was easy to use.

I would need technical support to use this system.

The various functions in this system were well integrated.

There was too much inconsistency in this system.

Most people would learn to use this system very quickly.

I found the system very cumbersome to use.

I felt very confident using the system.

I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system.

Scoring follows Brooke’s (1996) standard procedure: for odd-numbered items, the score contribution is the scale position minus 1; for even-numbered items, it is 5 minus the scale position. The sum of these contributions is multiplied by 2.5 to yield a score out of 100 for each respondent, and the mean across all respondents is reported as the system’s overall SUS score.
